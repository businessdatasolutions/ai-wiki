What this exposes

Three readings, and I don't think one of them wins

Scoring an instrument against the case its markers came from is a stress test, and this one produced a result worth arguing with rather than accepting. The readings are not mutually exclusive.

R1
The instrument is right

His evaluation layer really is the least-developed part

This is the reading I find hardest to dismiss, because it comes from his own account rather than from my scoring. He describes the draft-and-rewrite habit as a problem he had to engineer around. Everything he built to address it — the loop that finds unanswered drafts and diffs them against what he actually sent — is retrospective. It recovers the signal after the fact instead of setting the standard before it.

That matters because the wiki's own claim, from three independent directions, is that evaluation is the terminal skill — the thing that separates people who direct fleets of agents from people who thrive doing it. On that claim, an otherwise exceptional system with a thin evaluation layer is exactly what the scale should flag. It caught the real weak point.

R2
The gate is in the wrong place

G9 probably belongs at Level 5, not Level 4

Level 4 is defined by delegation: work runs without you, and the agent writes to things you rely on. Blum does both, emphatically. Written evaluation criteria are a compounding-grade practice — they sit naturally beside friction logging and distribution, which are already L5 gates. Putting them at L4 makes the delegation level depend on a discipline that most people reach later.

Move G9 to L5 and he scores Level 5 with one open gate — which matches both the shape of his system and the intuition of anyone watching the episode. That is a real defect in v1 and I would fix it before anyone used this on themselves.

R3
The rule is too brittle

Strict gating turns one partial answer into a two-level drop

Twelve of thirteen gates met, and the level is 3. That is the ladder logic working as designed — you are at the highest level whose gates you have all passed — but it means a single "sometimes, informally" outweighs eleven emphatic yeses. For a diagnostic that names one next action, strictness is a feature. For anything resembling a rating, it is a liability.

The honest fix is not partial credit, which would blur the gates back into a score. It is to report the level and the shape — "Level 3, with all four Level 5 gates met" — which the thin-base flag already does, and which the headline number does not.
