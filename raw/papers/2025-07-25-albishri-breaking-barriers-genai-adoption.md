Abstract
Purpose – This research aims to examine the adoption and continued use of generative artificial intelligence
among Saudi business executives, using the diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory framework. It addresses how
perceptions of relative advantage and compatibility influence Gen AI trialability and how trialability impacts
benefit observability, social influence and continued usage (CU).
Design/methodology/approach – The study is quantitative in nature and used a convenience sampling method
to collect data from 342 executives from diverse industries. The PLS-based structural equation modeling
method was used for analysis.
Findings – The study shows Gen AI’s relative advantages, like faster outcomes and workflow compatibility,
boost executives’ trialability, fostering observable benefits and social influence for wider adoption. While
complexity hinders trialability, compatibility mitigates its effects. Mediation analysis identifies trialability as
crucial in linking relative advantage and compatibility to CU. These insights guide effective Gen AI integration.
Originality/value – The study adds to the DOI theory by reporting the mediation effect of trialability and the
moderation effect of Gen AI complexity adopting advanced technology in organizations. The results provide
valuable insights to organizations that are considering adopting Gen AI.
Keywords Artificial intelligence, Generative AI, Technology, Organizational commitment,

2. Diffusion of innovation theory
The DOI theory by Rogers (2003), helps examine technological innovation and adoption by
the members of society (Bedu�e and Fritzsche, 2022; Li et al., 2024). According to Rogers
(2003), DOI is the transmission of innovation over a period among the individuals within a
social system (p.5). The four components that help to spread the innovation are the invention of
new technology (Gen AI), internal and external transmission channels, time, and social system
(Feuerriegel et al., 2024; Rogers, 2003). The theory depicts that novel technology or invention
is transmitted to various sections of society at different periods and is further accepted by the
larger part of the societal members.
The DOI theory is relevant for analyzing the adoption of Gen AI by industry professionals
to achieve organizational goals (Ishengoma and John, 2024). It outlines five stages of
adoption: knowledge (learning about the technology), persuasion (forming attitudes based on
advantages and peer influence), decision (evaluating benefits for acceptance or rejection),
implementation (training to optimize use), and confirmation (social validation reinforcing
adoption) (Cetindamar Kozanoglu and Abedin, 2021; Rogers et al., 1996; Rogers, 2003).
Rogers (2003, p. 232) describes innovation diffusion as an uncertainty reduction process,
where five attributes, namely, relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and
benefit observability, impact technology adoption within a social system. In the Saudi Arabian
industrial context where technical expertise and resources are immense, it is vital to examine
how Gen AI’s relative advantage provides a better solution to the existing organizational
problems and is compatible with the business executives’ learning skills. Organizations are
reluctant to use Gen AI technology due to its complexity and extensive training requirements
for implementation. Using Gen AI can sometimes produce biased or factually incorrect
outputs, raising trust and accountability issues. Further, the adoption of Gen AI is becoming
problematic due to the uncertainty about the return on investment and confusion and confusion
about the expected benefits. So, it is essential to study in the Saudi Arabian industrial context,
how trialability allows organizations to use Gen AI on a trial basis to reduce the uncertainty and
perceived risks associated with it and observe the benefits that enhance its CU. By using Gen
AI complexity as a moderator variable, the study examines how technological complexity
creates doubt in the minds of the business executives that influence the relationship between
(a) Gen AI’s relative advantage and (b) Gen AI compatibility with its trialability and adoption
in the organization.
3. Literature review and hypotheses development
3.1 Gen AI relative advantage (GenAI RA)
Rogers (2003) defined relative advantage as “the degree to which an innovation is perceived
as being better than the idea it supersedes” (p. 229). Within DOI theory, relative advantage
significantly influences technology adoption, as technologies with clear benefits are adopted
faster (Singh et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2023). Additionally, relative advantage directly links to
trialability, increasing adoption likelihood, and positively impacts organizational outcomes

While Gen AI enhances managerial engagement and performance, reluctance remains due to
ethical concerns and uncertainty about its organizational use (Manresa et al., 2024; Nah et al.,
2023; Singh et al., 2024). Factors influencing the adoption of new technology include
compatibility, infrastructure, knowledge, resources, culture, competition, regulation, and
ecosystem (Schwaeke et al., 2024). Given that users often feel apprehensive about embracing
new technologies (Wankhede et al., 2024), it becomes essential to explore how business
executives perceive the relative advantages of Gen AI and how trialability can promote its
adoption. Based on the above arguments, the study proposes the following hypothesis:
H1. The perceived relative advantage of Gen AI will positively impact its trialability.
3.2 Gen AI compatibility (Gen AI CompT)
Compatibility refers to how well new technology aligns with users’ attributes and usage
conditions (Bawack et al., 2021; Korzynski et al., 2023). Compatibility with Gen AI positively
influences its acceptance (Yudhistyra and Srinuan, 2024), with organizations favoring
technologies that complement existing offerings (Sahoo et al., 2023). Understanding drivers
motivating executives to adopt Gen AI is vital for achieving organizational goals. Personalized
Gen AI experiences may encourage executives to trial advanced tools. However, perceived
risks negatively affect users’ attitudes, behavioral control, and adoption intentions (Ivanov
et al., 2024). While the adoption of Gen AI represents a significant paradigm shift towards
intelligent systems, concerns regarding its integration persist (Gupta et al., 2024), thereby
inhibiting their adoption (Shore et al., 2024). We argue that the trialability of Gen AI is crucial
for employees, to enhance their confidence in advanced technology. Subsequently we
hypothesize:
H2.

The compatibility of Gen AI with existing technology will positively impact its
trialability.

3.3 Gen AI trialability (GenAT TRL)
Trialability (TRL) is “the extent to which an innovation can be tested on a daily basis”
(Rogers, 2003, p. 258). Introducing advanced technologies, such as Gen AI, often raises
doubts about their potential benefits and drawbacks among users (Aziz et al., 2024).
Organizations face challenges in ensuring the accuracy, correctness, data privacy, intellectual
property, and legal compliance of Gen AI generated content (Gupta et al., 2024). There is also
uncertainty about how well Gen AI will integrate into existing systems and workflows, and
whether it will deliver measurable results (Mariani and Dwivedi, 2024). Additionally, a lack
of internal expertise, potential resistance, and evolving regulatory frameworks complicate
adoption decisions (Mondal et al., 2023). The use of Gen AI technology on a trial basis is
essential for organizations to assess its observed benefits and to reduce the uncertainties and
perceived risk of its adoption (Rana et al., 2024). TRL allows organizations to experiment
with Gen AI technology on a small scale through pilot testing enabling them to understand
better or observe its benefits. Benefit observability, or the ability to see tangible outcomes
from Gen AI use (e.g. improved productivity, faster content creation, or enhanced decisionmaking), is crucial to build trust and demonstrate effectiveness within organizational
contexts (Rana et al., 2024).
TRL enhances positive belief and benefit observation, thus lowering the risk that
accompanies the adoption of new technology (Jilani et al., 2022) and increasing the probability
of successful integration (Ishengoma and John, 2024). Although advanced digital technologies
like AI and machine learning offer significant capabilities, it remains uncertain whether
businesses would benefit by embracing these innovations (Nafizah et al., 2024). There is a



need to investigate the effects of the trialability of Gen AI on the observability of the benefits
by business professionals. Accordingly, we hypothesize:
H3. The Gen AI trialability will positively impact the benefit observability of Gen AI.
Technology adoption is dependent on peer acceptance and attitudes and professionals are
likely to use colleagues’ opinions and ratings to mitigate risks (Oluyinka et al., 2021; Kumar
et al., 2025). The link between trialability and social influence is validated by empirical studies
(Oluyinka et al., 2021). We think that trialability will allow business leaders to try and
understand the benefits of Gen AI in detail because of which they will be able to say positive
things to their peers and colleagues and reduce the feeling of uncertainty around adoption of
Gen AI. Therefore, we hypothesize based on the preceding remarks.

H4. The Gen AI trialability will positively impact the social influence of Gen AI.
Organizations and business leaders frequently experiment with technological tools in a trial
basis to find out the value and effectiveness of the tools in supporting business goals (Oluyinka
et al., 2021). The first trial phase is crucial in determining whether or not they will incorporate
the technology into their usual operations (Xu et al., 2024). The attitude towards AI has a

adopt AI (Ishengoma and John, 2024). If the trial experience is positive, it can help build
confidence in the technology’s suitability to workplace needs and lead to further usage. We
also believe that trialability will not only help initial adoption but also promote long-term
technology integration. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed based on this rationale:
H5. The Gen AI trialability will positively impact the CU of Gen AI.
3.4 Gen AI benefit observability (GenAI BO)
Observability refers to the extent to which the outcomes of an advanced technology, such as
Gen AI, are noticeable and apparent to others (Bedu�e and Fritzsche, 2022). It reflects how
easily the technology can be perceived, conceptualized, or articulated by its users (Singh et al.,
2024). Previous research has emphasized that observability of benefits is critical in adoption of
new technology (Chatterjee et al., 2024).
According to Rogers’ (2003) DOI theory, observable technology outcomes significantly
influence adoption decisions. Gen AI’s ability to process diverse data and provide creative
solutions boosts its popularity (Henrickson and Mero~no-Pe~
nuela, 2023). Awareness of Gen AI
benefits is crucial in adoption and helps managers achieve business objectives (Chatterjee et al.,
2024). In competitive industrial markets where efficiency and tangible results matter, recognizing
the benefits of Gen AI generates confidence towards adoption. It is therefore critical to examine
how the observability of Gen AI benefits is linked to business executives’ continued adoption of
such tools. Based on these arguments, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H6. The benefit observability of Gen AI will positively impact the CU of Gen AI.
3.5 Gen AI perceived social influence (GenAI PSI)
Social influence refers to changes in beliefs or behavior due to input from knowledgeable
individuals (Cohen, 2013). Organizational culture, supportive policies, and colleagues’ input
shape employees’ openness to adopt new technologies. Attitudes toward technology depend
on knowledge and are influenced by technological uncertainty and social influence
(Boguslawski et al., 2024). Social influence, extensively studied in technology adoption,
helps employees and managers reduce uncertainty by relying on peers and leaders’ opinions.
Positive influence fosters trust, favorable attitudes, and integration into operations (Cao et al.,
2023; Rutatola et al., 2024). In group-oriented environments, where collective dynamics often

3.6 Gen AI complexity (Gen AI CompX) as a moderator
Complexity is “the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to
understand and use” (Rogers, 2003, p. 15). In Gen AI adoption, complexity relates to ease of
use, integration changes, and system alignment. High complexity hinders adoption by
discouraging workflow adjustments or extensive learning (Feuerriegel et al., 2024; Khan
et al., 2024). Despite this, Gen AI excels in solving business problems, analyzing large data,
and aiding decision-making with minimal human input (Khan et al., 2024).
For employees with relevant expertise, Gen AI complexity can encourage trialability rather
than hinder adoption. Compatibility with Gen AI fosters trust, engagement, and a positive attitude
despite complexity, boosting performance and adoption. However, managers remain reluctant,
citing early adoption stages and the need for more time to build confidence (Manresa et al., 2024).
Complexity of Gen AI is an important factor that can influence its trialability. However,
relative advantages of Gen AI, such as automation and performance optimization, can
motivate business executives to test the technology despite its complexity. Additionally,
compatibility with workplace needs and processes can encourage executives to try Gen AI,
despite its perceived complexity. To understand the impact of the complexity of AI we propose
the following hypotheses:
H8a. The Gen AI complexity positively moderates the relationship between Gen AI’s
relative advantage and Gen AI trialability, such as relative advantages of advanced
Gen AI technology motivate business executives to use it on a trial basis and check
its effectiveness.
H8b.

Gen AI complexity positively moderates the relationship between Gen AI
compatibility and Gen AI trialability, such as Gen AI compatibility motivates
business executives to use it on a trial basis and check its effectiveness.

4. Research methods
4.1 The measurement instrument
Established scales from prior research were adapted to the context of this study to gather data
regarding the constructs of interest (Table 1). The scales from Moore and Benbasat (1991) and
Wang et al. (2008) were used for measuring Gen AI’s relative advantage, while scales for Gen
AI compatibility and Gen AI BO were adapted from the study of Raman et al. (2024). The
scale items to calibrate Gen AI trialability were adopted from Lin and Bautista (2017), Gen AI
PSI from Garf-Vlachy et al. (2017), Gen AI complexity from Idoga et al. (2022), and Gen AI
CU intention from Lin and Yu (2023) study. A 7-point Likert-type scale- 1 (strongly disagree)
to 7 (strongly agree)- response format was used during the measurement.
Since the original scales employed in all above-mentioned studies were not framed in other
contexts of technology adoption, the authors rephrased them to align with the context of GenAI
adoption. For example, an item from Kaur et al. (2020) scale to measure compatibility in the
context of adoption of m-wallets was rephrased from “I think that using an m-wallet fits well
with the way I like to buy” to “Using Generative AI fits well with my purpose of learning”.
A follow-up content assessment was also conducted by gathering feedback from ten
senior and mid-level executives who evaluated the questionnaire. Revisions were made based
on the insights received from these industry professionals. Adaptations were made to enhance
clarity in question phrasing. Given that the quality (validity and reliability) of all these scales
has been robustly established through prior studies over the years, and since we did not alter
them, pilot testing was not conducted. However, before subjecting the data to the final
analyses, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed along with the standard data
checks.

4.2 Participants and procedures
This study’s sampled population consisted of executives holding multiple managerial roles
across different Saudi organizations. These executives were considered suitable participants
based on their inclination towards adopting modern digital tools, including GenAI in their
working routines (Ali and Dammak, 2025). Since a sampling frame for such a population was
unavailable, a non-probability sampling method, convenience (snowball) sampling, was used
to identify the relevant informants. Convenience sampling focuses on including respondents
based on their availability, accessibility, geographic proximity, or willingness to participate
(Etikan, 2016). One of the authors from Saudi Arabia approached representative industry
professionals and requested them to share the link to the Google Forms progressively with the
people in their contact. The settings in the Google Forms allowed a participant to respond only
once to the questionnaire. The survey instructions also explicitly asked respondents to
participate only once. This was done to remove duplicity of responses. Furthermore, to
minimize the chances of data clustering from same department, authors ensured that all the
departments in an organization were approached, and responses were collected. Following
Habib et al. (2025), to determine the representativeness of our sample, the authors compared it
to the Saudi national statistics on the demographics of individuals (respondents) and firms
provided by Statista (2025). The statistics were more accessible and thus comparable at the
firm level. As can been seen in Table 2, The majority of respondents were males (67.3%), held
graduation degrees (55.8%), were under 30 years old (45.9%), worked for firms less than
10 years old (60.5%), and were employed at firms with fewer than 250 employees (37.1%).
These percentiles are comparable to the national statistics regarding the composition of the
general population, labor market, and industry composition, establishing the
representativeness of this study’s sample. Our sample includes a broad range of managerial
roles, from entry-level and first-line managers to senior executives. This reflects the evolving
managerial landscape within Saudi organizations, particularly in innovative and high-growth
sectors, where younger professionals may occupy executive positions.

Harman’s single-factor test was performed following the guidelines of Podsakoff et al.

factor analysis on all items to determine if a single factor that explains a significant portion
(>50%) of the variance emerges. The results indicated that the unrotated single latent factor
accounted for less than 50% of the variance, suggesting the lack of significance of the common
method variance (CMV). The measured latent marker variable (MLMV) approach was also
applied, as recommended by Chang et al. (2020). “Firm Size”, a variable distinct from the
primary variables in the study, has been designated a marker variable. MLMV methods partials
out CMV by regressing each measurement item on the entire set of MLMV items, using the
resultant change in R2 as an indicator of CMV. In our case the R2 values were computed and
compared using models with and without MLMV. The R2 values of the endogenous constructs
remained relatively unchanged following the inclusion of the MLMV, indicating that the
model was not affected by confounding effects attributable to the CMV (Chang et al., 2020).
Soper’s a priori method was used to obtain a statistically appropriate sample size,
considering an effect size of 0.3 (Cohen, 2013), 0.80 statistical power, and a 0.01 significance
level, with a solution consisting of six constructs calibrated through 30 indicators. Based on
these parameters, a sample of 203 informants was deemed appropriate. Therefore, the final
sample size (n 5 342) was considered sufficient and suitable for evaluating the proposed

4.3 Analytical strategy
The analysis utilized PLS-based structural equation modeling (SEM) with SmartPLS 4.0
(Ringle et al., 2024) to evaluate the measurement and structural models. Initially, the reliability
and validity of the measurement scales were ascertained through the CFA. Following this, the
structural model was tested to examine the significance of the hypothesized relationships. This
modeling technique has favored alternative methods because of the study’s complex model,
which includes direct effects, indirect effects, and sequential mediation processes that SEM is
particularly well-suited to assess. Additionally, partial least squares structural equation
modeling (PLS-SEM) emphasizes prediction, making it particularly suitable for the
exploratory objectives of this study. Finally, unlike other techniques, PLS-SEM does not
require the assumption of data normality and is more appropriate for models that include
formative constructs (Hair et al., 2022).

5.1 Assessment of the measurement model
5.1.1 Scales’ reliability and validity. The strength (threshold 5 0.7) and significance of all 30
individual items comprising six constructs were examined to assess their reliability. In line
with Raykov’s (2016) guidelines, items with insignificant or low factor loadings or high
multicollinearity were systematically removed based on their factor loading values. After each
removal, the α (if deleted), Rho_a, and average variance extracted were examined to ensure
that reliability and validity were not compromised. The reliability at the construct level was
assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α) and composite reliability with the 0.7 thresholds
suggested by Hair et al. (2022). As can be seen in Table 3, items/constructs comprehensively
surpassed the recommended thresholds.
Finally, discriminant validity was tested by applying Fornell and Larcker’s (1981) test
where the root of all AVEs should be higher than the inter-construct correlations. The former
was also established based on the analysis of the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratios.

6. Discussion
To address RQ1, H1 and H2 revealed a positive relationship between Gen AI’s relative
advantage, compatibility, and executives’ willingness to try it. This aligns with prior research
showing relative advantage motivates trial adoption (Chatterjee et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024;
Jilani et al., 2022). While Gen AI enhances workplace performance, reluctance due to trust
persists (Manresa et al., 2024; Brougham and Haar, 2020).

alignment with existing systems and employee readiness encourages experimentation. This
aligns with Yudhistyra and Srinuan (2024), who found compatibility boosts adoption intent,
but contrasts with Ivanov et al. (2024), who highlighted perceived risks reducing adoption.
The findings confirm that compatibility supports trial usage (Bader and Kaiser, 2019). The
study investigates how Gen AI trialability influences BO, PSI, and continued use (RQ2: H3H5). Results reveal trial use lowers risk perception and encourage adoption (Jilani et al., 2022).

7. Implications
7.1 Theoretical implications
This study validates DOI variables in the context of Saudi executives’ continued Gen AI usage,
highlighting relative advantage and compatibility as key drivers of trialability, which impacts
continued use. It extends DOI theory by emphasizing trialability as a critical mediator that
reduces uncertainty and builds confidence, aligning with Rogers’ view of trialability reducing
adoption risks. The study extends DOI theory by highlighting complexity’s moderating role in
Gen AI adoption. While excessive complexity hinders trialability and adoption, compatibility
with the technology mitigates its adverse effects. These findings refine DOI theory, showing
complexity’s impact is context-dependent and influenced by individual or organizational
readiness. Non-compatibility with Gen AI limits employees’ autonomy and willingness to use
it. However, organizations that support and allow time for adaptation achieve better results
(Bader and Kaiser, 2019). This study expands DOI theory by showing that Gen AI trialability
enhances PSI, fostering positive attitudes. Through mediation and serial mediation models, it
integrates DOI components, providing a comprehensive view of innovation diffusion in
organizations.
7.2 Practical implications
The study highlights that perceived relative advantage and compatibility drive Gen AI trial
adoption by executives. Gen AI enhances efficiency through improved task performance,
while compatibility with skills, work, and environment supports adoption. According to



Meijer et al. (2021) organizations should make their employees aware of Gen AI benefits and
initiate Gen AI trials in accordance with their workforce skill before widespread adoption
since without the employees’ compatibility with the Gen AI they will not take the full
advantage of technology. Conversely, the non-compatibility of technology creates a
technological cage around them, negatively impacting their performance efficiency. So, it is
essential for the organizations first to skill their employees and then use the technology on a
trial basis to observe its full benefits.
This research underlines the need for adequate training and development for business
executives. Developing technologically related skills can help achieve career-related tasks and
impact organizational performance (Chatterjee et al., 2024). To accelerate the usage of
advanced technological tools like Gen AI on a trial basis, develop employees’ trust in the
technology. This helps them change their attitude and risk perception towards the technology,
and improve their CU (Ivanov et al., 2024).
Gen AI complexity moderates the relationship between Gen AI compatibility and
trialability. Technological compatibility with the employees reduces the effects of the
complexity. This factor shows the importance of training and educational development of the
business executives, which makes them capable to use the complex technology to benefit
the organization and creates the competitive advantage for them. It is suggested that
orientation endeavors should acclimatize the employees to the use of the technology with a
focus on their relative benefits as a way of motivation and containment of fear.
The study reports that Gen AI trialability mediates the relationship between relative
advantage and compatibility with business executives’ continued technology usage. This
result depicts the importance and relative advantage of Gen AI for business executives.
If executives understand the benefits of technology, then their interest will automatically shift
to the technology, and they will start using it on a continued basis. Similarly, their compatibility
with the Gen AI positively influences their adoption of technology on trial basis, further
reducing the technological uncertainties and building their trust in advanced technology
(Gangwar et al., 2025).
FS and YOO did not affect Gen AI trialability, BO, or continued use of Gen AI. This
underscores the need to be a first mover when it comes to adopting new technologies into
business operations. Early adopters can increase their competitive advantages more than late
adopters or laggards (Nah et al., 2023). This provides a valuable lesson for organizations: the
use of technological developments like Gen AI must not be restricted by the firm’s size or the
firm’s experience in the industry. Instead, businesses must proactively embrace innovation and
train employees to use the new technology effectively.

8. Limitations and future scope
This study possesses certain limitations that can be addressed in future research. This crosssectional study collected data from company executives at a single moment, potentially
leading to causality errors (i.e. endogeneity issues) among constructs. In future research, the
investigators may analyze the same issue utilizing longitudinal data. Similarly, the study used a
convenience sampling method to collect data from business executives, which can be replaced
with probability sampling techniques in future. It may also cause common method issues
attributable to self-reporting, similar items usage, consistency, and social desirability
inclinations. Introducing time-lagged data using disparate measurement scales for different
constructs and using multiple informants can help mitigate issues stemming from CMB.
Secondly, the research has gathered data from corporate executives across several industries in
Saudi Arabia. This may lead to the problem of generalized outcomes and future research can
broaden the scope of the existing study by incorporating cross-industry comparisons and
exploring regional differences. By examining a diverse range of industries and regions,
researchers can gain an in-depth understanding of the factors influencing the adoption and
