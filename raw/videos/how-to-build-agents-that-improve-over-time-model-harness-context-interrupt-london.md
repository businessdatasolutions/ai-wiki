description: |-
  Harrison Chase, Co-Founder and CEO of LangChain, splits an agent into three parts, the model, the harness, and the context, and explains how each one can improve over time: the model's weights through supervised fine-tuning with LangSmith Fine-Tuning and its smithtune CLI, or through RL for more complex tasks; the harness through LangSmith Engine, a deep agent that runs over traces, clusters issues, and proposes fixes with a human in the loop; and the context through memory, where he points to Hex and Profound as companies he thinks do this well and to LangChain's open source OpenWiki project. He argues that traces and evals are the common foundation of all three loops, which is why LangChain built SmithDB and the new LangSmith AI Search for trace data, then covers what makes evals hard (realistic inputs, defining what good looks like, simulation environments) and how red teaming in LangSmith Engine looks for agent mistakes proactively. He closes with what he calls a North Star vision: an agent that notices its own mistakes, creates its own eval data points, and suggests its own fixes.

## [9:11] Why traces and evals are the foundation of improvement

[9:11] So what's common across all of these three things? We think there's two things that are common, traces and evals. And we think these are the core foundation of this improvement loop, and it's where we spend a lot of time.
[9:23] This improvement loop may look different for different use cases and different verticals. The hardest part of the improvement loop is oftentimes choosing what to remember and what not to remember and how to remember.

[12:01] You can query what's going on. But next comes evals. So evals are really important for doing this loop for a few reasons.

## [12:02] Why evals are the new PRD

[12:07] First, they're the new PRD. And so this is a saying that I really like, where you might have a product requirements document describing what the product should do.
[12:16] In AI, there's so many edge cases that the best way to describe that oftentimes is in the form of evals.
[12:23] So evals are where you define what good looks like. And so good can mean you respond in a certain way to certain queries. It can mean you don't respond to certain queries. Defining the limits, defining what good looks like is a really important part of evals and a really important part of defining how you want to improve. And so this is kind of like the guardrails that are going to keep you in the safe zone as you iterate in this improvement loop.

## [12:50] Why is building evals so hard?

[12:50] Building evals is really, really hard. This is one of the main things that we work with a bunch of folks on. There's not really a silver bullet for evals out there. Sure, we can provide infrastructure for running evals and visualizing eval results.
[13:03] But evals need to be domain-specific, both the datasets that they run on and the evaluators that are used. And so we still think building evals is really hard.
[13:12] There's different parts of this. So first, you need to come up with realistic inputs. And so this can often be hard.
[13:18] You might not know what people will even ask your agent. Sometimes they'll go completely off topic. Sometimes they'll go orthogonally or close to it.
[13:26] But you may not be able to predict all these cases up front. And so even being able to just enumerate what realistic inputs are is not as easy as it sounds. And this is why tracing is so important. You need to be able to define what a good answer looks like.
[13:38] There's different ways that we've seen people do this, maybe calling out kind of like three off the top of my head. One, you can define what the expected output is, a certain number that it should mention, an exact kind of like phrase that it should use.
[13:53] This is kind of called an exact match. This gets complicated for more complex agents, because it's usually hard to do that.
[14:00] And so what we see people do is then defining criteria. Basically, this agent should have part of the answer that mentions this.
[14:08] This answer should not say this. It could be positive or negative criteria. These criteria could act on either the final answer itself or the environment that the agent's running in.
