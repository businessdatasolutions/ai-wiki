## [0:00] Five terms, one confusion

[0:00] The word ontology tends to arrive in a meeting with no explanation attached, usually alongside
[0:05] taxonomy, knowledge graph, semantic model, and semantic layer, and by the end of the hour, people are using all five as if they were names for one idea.
[0:14] They are related, yet each one answers its own question, and mixing them up is how teams end up buying or building the wrong thing.
[0:23] What an ontology gives you, that others don't, is mainly a way to describe the kinds of things your business deals with and the rules about how they relate.
[0:32] And once that makes sense, the other four fall into place around it.

## [0:37] Start with a sport: rulebook vs scoreboard

[0:37] Start with a sport. Since most people know how one works, a rulebook for soccer tells you what kinds of things exist in a game, like players, goalkeepers, referees, and goals.
[0:47] It tells you how they relate.
[0:49] So a goalkeeper is a kind of a player and a goal is scored when the whole ball crosses the goal line between the post and under the bar.
[0:57] And it tells you rules such as that a team can have at most 11 players on the field and one of them has to be the goalkeeper.
[1:05] A scoreboard is something different. It records what happened in one particular match like which player scored in minute 32.
[1:12] The rulebook describes the world in general and the scoreboard describes one instance of it.
[1:18] An ontology is the rulebook and a knowledge graph is closer to the scoreboard. In data terms, picture a university.

## [1:23] A university as an ontology

[1:26] It has students, instructors, courses, departments, and the thing students sign up for, which is a section, meaning one offering of a course in a particular term.
[1:37] An ontology for this would list the kind of things which are called classes, such as person, student, instructor, course, and section.
[1:45] It would define the relationships between them, such as an instructor teaches a section. A section is an offering of a course.
[1:53] A student enrolls in a section, and it would add rules. So a graduate teaching assistant is both a student and an employee.
[2:00] A section belongs to exactly one course, and prerequisite is transitive, which means
[2:06] that if calculus 1 is a prerequisite for calculus 2 and calculus 2 is a prerequisite
[2:12] for calculus 3, then calculus 1 is also required indirectly for calculus 3 without anyone having to write that third fact down.

## [2:21] What the formal side is for

[2:21] That last example shows what the formal side is for.
[2:25] The standard languages for writing ontologies such as OWL from the World Wide Web Consortium, the
[2:30] group behind many web standards are built so that software called a reasoner can work out facts that nobody stated from the rules that were written down.
[2:40] Tell it that every graduate assistant is a student and a student enrolled in a section cannot also be its instructor and it can flag a record that breaks the rule.
[2:50] The classic definition from a computer scientist named Tom Gruber in 1993 is
[2:55] that an ontology is an explicit specification of a conceptualization, which sounds heavy but comes down to this.
[3:03] It's a written, agreed description of the things that exist in some area and the relationships between them. Now the neighbors.

## [3:10] Taxonomy and knowledge graph

[3:11] So a taxonomy is a hierarchy of categories where each thing falls under a broader one, like person, then student, then graduate student.
[3:20] It captures only one relationship, the one that says a thing is a kind of something
[3:26] broader. An ontology includes hierarchy but adds the other relationships like teaches and enrolls in and the rules.
[3:35] A knowledge graph stores facts as a network of things and links between them.
[3:41] So each fact is a small sentence with a subject, a relationship, and an object such
[3:46] as Maya enrolled in section two of statistics and the facts join up into
[3:52] a web where every student and section is a point and every relationship is a line
[3:57] between two points. Google popularized the term in 2012 when it announced
[4:03] its knowledge graph as a way for search to understand the things people ask about beyond
[4:08] matching their words. The ontology and the graph work together, since the ontology says what
[4:14] kinds of things and links are allowed and the knowledge graph holds the particular students and sections. A graph can be built without a formal ontology, but then nothing checks whether it
[4:24] makes sense. Next, the two that data teams are more likely to have heard of, a semantic model. A semantic model is the set of business definitions tied to your tables,

## [4:25] Semantic model and semantic layer

[4:35] like what counts as an enrolled student and precisely how the headcount is calculated so everyone who asks get the same number.
[4:43] A semantic layer is the service that serves those definitions to dashboards and increasingly to AI agents.
[4:51] For this comparison, I would put it this way. A semantic model and its layers are mainly about metrics and the data underneath them.
[4:59] So they answer how many students enrolled, calculated which way. An ontology is mainly about the kinds of things and how they connect.
[5:08] So it answers what a student is, how that relates to an employee, and what follows from the rules. A good deal of the time they work side by side, with the ontology informing the entities and relationships in the semantic model.

## [5:23] Why this matters for AI

[5:23] Why has this come up so much since AI arrived?
[5:26] Think about a large language model, an AI system trained on huge amounts of text answering a question about the university.
[5:33] It can guess from the column names that a table of enrollment links students to sections, but it's guessing and it may not know that a teaching assistant shows up in both the student system and the payroll system as the same person.
[5:47] An ontology gives it the relationships and constraints written down.
[5:51] And a 2023 benchmark from a data catalog vendor found GPT -4 answered far more database questions correctly when it worked through a knowledge graph.

## [6:02] Keeping an ontology useful

[6:02] Ontology is something people have to maintain. Somebody has to own the definition of student, decide whether a new kind of thing belongs
[6:09] in the model, and record when the meaning changed, which is data governance work and not only modeling work. A few things go wrong.
[6:18] One is trying to model the whole enterprise at once when it's better to start with one question the business cannot answer today.
[6:26] Another is calling a category tree an ontology so you get the label without the relationships
[6:31] and rules. A third is writing a beautiful ontology that never gets connected to the tables, so it stays as documentation and no query or agent ever uses it.
[6:41] And the fourth is that nobody owns it, so it drifts away from how the business uses its words. So an ontology is the agreed rule book for what exists in your business and how it connects.

## [6:45] The whole picture in one line

[6:53] The knowledge graph holds the particular facts, And the semantic model and layer pin down the business numbers and serve them to whoever asks.
