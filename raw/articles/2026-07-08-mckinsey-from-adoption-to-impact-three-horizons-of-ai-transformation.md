# From adoption to impact: Three horizons of AI transformation (structured notes)

*McKinsey Quarterly*, article, 8 July 2026. Dek: most organizations are still early in their AI journeys; a new global survey shows how companies can move from individual adoption to enterprise-wide value capture.

## Research method (sidebar "About the research", and endnotes 1–3, 5)

- **Instrument:** "AI Individual & Organizational Readiness Assessment Panel Survey".
- **Sample:** 750 English-speaking employees across job levels in North America, Europe, Asia–Pacific, Latin America, and Eastern Europe/Middle East/Africa.
- **Fieldwork:** February to April 2026.
- **Screen:** respondents had to report incorporating AI into their work.
- **Content:** organizational and personal AI readiness, enterprise value, selected questions from McKinsey's Organizational Health Index, and other AI questions (for example, anxiety about AI).
- **Unit of analysis:** individual responses. The sidebar states they are not representative accounts of the respondents' organizations.
- **Leaders only (608):** executives, senior leaders and middle managers answered the organizational-readiness and enterprise-value questions. Personal readiness covers all 750.
- **Horizon classification:** 717 respondents were reliably classified into one horizon, at their highest qualifying level. Within-horizon results on readiness and value use 582 leaders.
- **Recruitment bias, stated:** recruitment targeted respondents from organizations in more advanced horizons, to ensure every horizon was represented. The sidebar warns the estimates therefore *"may not reflect overall market prevalence."*
- **Endnote 1:** respondents judged the horizon for their whole organization (85%), their unit or department (11%), or their work group or project team (4%).
- **Endnote 2:** "enterprise value impact" is a composite of four questions on measurable improvement from AI: bottom line, cost savings, employee experience, customer outcomes.
- **Endnote 3:** job levels were executive team 15%, top management 35%, middle management 31%, frontline management 13%, individual contributor or frontline worker 6%. Only the first three answered the organizational-readiness and value questions.
- **Endnote 5:** only leaders were surveyed on value capture. The authors say the findings are consistent with other McKinsey research, *"suggesting broader workforce relevance."*
- **Exhibit notes:** a "ready" or "high" respondent has a mean score of 4 or more on a composite; every question was on a 5-point agreement scale. The per-horizon exhibits show statistically significant positive predictors, from separate regression models for the organizational and personal readiness concepts.

## Section notes (paraphrased, with short quotes)

**Opening.** AI tops many technology budgets, but results trail ambitions. The authors' explanation: more people using AI does not by itself create enterprise value, because individual gains rarely compound when the organization around them is unchanged. Two things set the leaders apart. They concentrate on the highest-value areas and rewire workflows there, instead of spreading pilots or automating current processes. And they run AI as organizational change (workflows, behaviors, skills, leadership practices, change management), not as a technology deployment.

**The readiness gap.** Personal readiness, as defined, covers more than adoption: feeling supported, capable and willing; having tools; trusting the organization to support continual change. Organizational readiness is the leaders' view of whether the organization can make the people and culture shifts to become an *"agentic enterprise"*. That includes workflow redesign, leader AI fluency, resourcing new ways of working (including strategic workforce planning), and reshaping norms. Headline: 70% personally ready against 27% organizationally ready. The authors call this a sign that employees adapt faster than their institutions, citing McKinsey's *Superagency* report (endnote 4).

**Which readiness matters more.** Organizational readiness *"accounts for 48 percent"* of the difference between leaders who report capturing value and those who do not; personal readiness accounts for 25%. The authors read this as organizational readiness being nearly twice as important. The statistical method behind the 48/25 split is not stated on the page. They separate individual AI adoption at scale (common) from AI-enabled transformation (rare). The latter changes how work, decisions, team structure and value creation happen.

**Leaders.** "Bringing employees along" has to mean institutional behavior change, not encouragement to use tools. The organizations creating most value reshape norms, workflows, decision rights, roles and structures, resource allocation and performance expectations. Trust in leaders matters as much as trust in the technology. Four leader questions: where AI will create value; how work must change to capture it; what skills the leader must build; how to manage a human-and-agents system. Senior leaders need enough AI fluency to set a vision; bottom-up experimentation alone will not produce these choices.

**Reinvention is distant.** Nearly 90% of organizations are in the first two horizons. Leaders reporting enterprise value: 48% in reinvention, 24% in automation, 13% in enablement. Leaders saying their organization is not ready for the people and culture shifts: 84% in enablement, 68% in automation, 44% in reinvention.

**Trust across all horizons.** Trust in the organization is the one readiness factor that matters in every horizon. Anxiety about AI-related change appears at all levels. Middle managers report it most: one in four, against one in five individual contributors. Respondents with low trust in organizational support are 1.5 times as likely to feel anxious (endnote 7: a statistically significant negative relationship between AI anxiety and trust). The authors separate reducing anxiety from building trust. Reassurance that jobs will not change may lower anxiety for a while without building trust, if employees doubt it can hold. They recommend saying what is known and unknown, explaining how decisions will be made, following through, investing in capabilities, and redeploying talent. Organizations with higher trust report more value. Endnote 6 cites Edelman (2025).

**Horizon 1, enablement.** Typical use is drafting presentations, summarizing meetings and preparing for client interactions. Leaders are 5.3 times as likely to report enterprise value when workflows are redesigned (32% vs 6%). Daily work adaptation is the strongest personal-readiness factor. Two challenges: moving from experimentation to sustained use, and rewiring work while shifting behavior. Freed-up time often goes to personally interesting work not tied to enterprise priorities. The fix is to adjust workflows formally, set expectations for working differently, and channel freed capacity to business priorities. The skills focus is baseline fluency, not advanced capability.

**Horizon 2, automation.** End-to-end cross-functional processes are automated with AI; an example is AI routing customer requests, generating responses and escalating complex cases to humans. These organizations start from the current state and optimize it, which keeps them out of reinvention. Value drivers: a systems-level vision for redesigning the organization, a road map anchored in business value, active reallocation of resources, and changes to structure and formal roles. These can create perceived winners and losers, and resistance among some leaders. High leadership AI fluency: 3.9 times as likely to report value (35% vs 9%). Leaders receiving support and training to build new skills: 3.3 times (30% vs 9%).

**Horizon 3, reinvention.** Work, roles, workflows and operating models are redesigned from scratch. The constraint is increasingly leaders' ability to imagine new ways of working. Organizational behavior shifts, plus personal trust, are most associated with value. The authors then compared reinvention with enablement on seven AI-agnostic behaviors from the Organizational Health Index (endnote 8: employee innovation, knowledge sharing, performance transparency, process-based capabilities, talent development, tech enablement, top-down innovation). Four themes stood out:
1. Tech investments are business-led, with a clear link to value. Individual productivity with technology does not predict organizational health (endnote 9).
2. Innovation is championed from the top and spread horizontally by employees at all levels.
3. Rigorous performance transparency and regular process documentation (endnote 10, McKinsey's "Rigor" article).
4. Cross-functional collaboration with technology teams, plus continuous learning that stresses human skills: creative and analytical thinking, resilience, flexibility.

**Close: "Don't wait. Iterate."** The electrification analogy: factories swapped steam engines for electric motors but kept their layouts and management, and gains stayed small until factories were redesigned around electricity. AI is at the same point; layering it onto existing workflows will not transform results. A follow-up article with examples of leaders driving the change is promised.
