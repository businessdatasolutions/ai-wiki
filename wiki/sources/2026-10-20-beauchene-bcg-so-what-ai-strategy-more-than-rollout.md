# Beauchene (The So What from BCG) — your AI strategy must be more than a rollout

> Vinciane Beauchene explains why AI strategy, not tool access, drives value. Employees with plenty of tools but weak strategic guidance are five times less likely to see measurable impact. She explains how leaders can redesign work end-to-end, decide where to protect human value, and set clear guardrails as AI agents take on more. Adoption is an individual metric. Value creation is a systemic one.

## TL;DR

A 19-minute episode of *The So What from BCG* on the [[Boston Consulting Group]] channel, published 20 October 2026. Host **Georgie Frost** talks with **Vinciane Beauchene**, a former BCG managing director and partner who has since joined Schneider Electric as Chief AI Transformation Officer, about BCG's *AI at Work* survey. Five claims:

1. **Strategy beats tools, five to one.** An employee with strong access to AI tools but weak strategic guidance is *"five times less"* likely to get measurable impact than one with limited tools and strong guidance on where to use them and where to redeploy the time.
2. **"Adoption is an individual metric. Value creation is really a systemic output."** *"If you have five employees that each… get the 20% productivity, it does not mean that you will unleash one FTE,"* and improving one step only moves *"the bottleneck in the value chain."*
3. **Two archetypes.** Firms that treated AI as a tech deployment, against firms that chose a few processes to transform. The second got more impact *and* more employee satisfaction: the two *"reinforce each other."*
4. **Calling AI a coworker lowers accountability.** In a BCG experiment, people told an output came from an *"AI worker"* rather than an *"AI tool"* took less personal accountability and spotted fewer mistakes.
5. **Ask where to protect human value.** As AI becomes able to do most tasks, the strategic question becomes *"where should I protect human value add,"* which she expects to be a competitive differentiator.

## Key claims

### What the survey shows

Three in four employees now use AI regularly, *"the silicon ceiling has exploded,"* and regular users report saving about a day a week and enjoying work more, with tasks shifting toward *"more complex decision-making, more managing, less doing."* They also report a paradox: more satisfaction and *"increased cognitive strain."* Time saved *"cannot be the objective of your AI transformation. It's just an input."* Measure value per outcome or per process, and track human metrics alongside it: whether skills are holding or employees are *"de-skilling, which would create issues down the road,"* and the level of trust and confidence.

### Redesigning teams, and testing the human-AI split

In software development, AI first augmented coders, giving *"only incremental gains."* Agentic engineering then changed the whole lifecycle: specialised back-end and front-end roles fade into full-stack engineers, and the line between engineer and product owner blurs, *"because suddenly product owners can prototype."* BCG is experimenting with team shapes, a product owner and developer as *"a power couple"* or larger teams with one product owner, because *"it's very difficult to have the answer up front."*

Her second example is the clearest result in the episode. For a fashion company's sales forecast, AI forecast better than people. Letting experienced people revise the AI's forecast when it looked odd *"actually destroyed the reliability of the forecast compared to AI alone."* The best results came when people supplied context up front, *"some context elements that the AI cannot have access to,"* and then left the AI to do the job. *"What's the optimal human-AI interaction is really something that you cannot… predict up front. It's really something that you need to play with before you scale."*

### Where not to use AI

Frost raises Bill Gates's idea of a *human reserve*. Beauchene expects *"what can AI do?"* to become obsolete because AI will be able to do most of it; the question becomes where to deploy it and *"where should I protect human value add."* That is an ethical and regulatory question, but *"much more than that… a competitive advantage question"*: where a company keeps the human touch becomes part of its brand. Agents raise the stakes: *"any ambiguity in your strategy can also be much faster executed,"* so each strategic decision needs clarity on what agents may do, who is accountable, and the guardrails.

### Coworker or tool

Framing matters for *"ownership, authority, accountability, trust."* In BCG's experiment, people who believed an output came from an AI worker *"were just less attentive because… they associated it to a colleague and trusted it more,"* and fears about job security and identity were stronger. The coworker framing may help adoption, *"but adoption is not the issue anymore. It's more about driving value and the right level of quality."*

### What good looks like, and what to do now

Every employee should be able to answer three questions: where is the company going and how does AI contribute; what does that mean for my job and the value expected from me as a human; and how will I grow in that direction. The worst mistake for a leader is *"moving very fast and hard coding a number of operating model decisions,"* because the technology will keep changing. Design for change instead: minimise interdependencies between teams, give a few leaders accountability for end-to-end processes, and build a muscle for regular upskilling. Next steps: no *"peanut butter spreading,"* start from outcomes and redesign work at the system level, and build a transformation team that connects *"technology, business, strategy, HR, finance, risk."*
