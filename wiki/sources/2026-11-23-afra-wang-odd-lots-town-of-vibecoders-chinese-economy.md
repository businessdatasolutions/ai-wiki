# Afra Wang on Odd Lots — What a Town of Vibecoders Says About the Chinese Economy (Nov 2026)

> According to Stanford's AI Index, 84% of people surveyed in China say they are optimistic about AI. In the US, by contrast, 38% of those surveyed say they are optimistic. That's a big gulf in enthusiasm, and you can see it borne out in the stories you read: While in the US we hear about AI doomsday scenarios, in China, you'll hear about grandmas who are obsessed with using OpenClaw. Afra Wang has spent a lot of time in mainland China reporting from the country's AI hotbed — in a vibecoding-obsessed town outside of Hangzhou; at the for-profit schools where influencers are teaching everyday people how to use the technology; and the content studios that are pumping out AI microdramas. She understands, better than most, how AI is reshaping everyday life in China, and for today's episode, we talk to her about her reporting on AI in China and how the technology is actually diffusing into Chinese society.
>
> — channel description, Bloomberg Podcasts (links and show boilerplate omitted)

## TL;DR

A ~62-minute episode of *Odd Lots* on the **[[Bloomberg Podcasts]]** channel, published 23 November 2026. Hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway interview **Afra Wang**, a journalist covering AI in China and Silicon Valley. She describes three recent pieces, in *The New Yorker* and *New York* magazine among others, as a trilogy on how AI spreads through Chinese society: a vibe-coding town outside Hangzhou, the AI education industry, and AI-generated short dramas. The transcript is of speech-recognition quality; names were corrected at acquire (see the raw file's `notes:`). Her figures are her own reporting and are not checked here.

1. **China is running a different race.** Wang calls China's race *"full stack"*: electricity, the supply chain, and diffusion into industry, rather than a sprint to the frontier. *"Both countries are running on their own race."* The state's line, in Xi Jinping's speeches, is the **AI Plus** plan, modelled on the earlier Internet Plus plan: AI applied to factories, education and agriculture, framed as *"new quality productive forces."* She says China *"has yet to wake up"* to the risk of artificial general intelligence. She also reports her own recent spell of what she calls *"RSI psychosis"*, belief in imminent recursive self-improvement.
2. **The DeepSeek moment was a national event.** Before DeepSeek R1, Chinese tech workers posted that *"China cannot innovate."* R1 was trained on native Chinese text and felt Chinese rather than translated. It could quote Tang poetry. It was received as a sign of *guoyun*, national destiny, a term Xi uses.
3. **Why Chinese people are more optimistic about AI.** She cites 83% in China against 38% in the US who see AI as beneficial (the description gives 84%, from Stanford's AI Index). She gives two reasons. One: *"what choice do they have?"* There is no organised way to refuse, and AI is presented as an inevitable step in China's upgrading. Two: past technology waves visibly improved lives. Her 81-year-old grandmother once walked five hours to buy a clock; now she commands smart bulbs by voice and chats with Doubao.
4. **The vibe-coding town.** The town, outside Hangzhou (the wiki reads the name as Liangzhu), has cheap rents and a slow pace. It draws laid-off big-tech workers, people tired of the *"996"* grind, and graduates who skip job hunting. They become one-person companies and independent [[vibe-coding|vibe coders]]. Most apps are whimsical: tools for ADHD or dyslexia, mental-health and focus apps, five to ten fortune-telling apps. Asked about users, founders answer *"three, seven, eight."* The one profitable astrology app she wrote about, FateTell, checks bazi compatibility before hiring. Its founder invited her to the office opening because her *"fire"* would balance the room.
5. **AI as upward mobility, and as a pyramid.** For young people facing a harsh job market, AI looks like a way up. She gives youth unemployment as 18.9% officially, and doubts the figure. Many vibe coders earn their money by selling AI classes, not apps. She visited one of the largest AI education firms, whose name translates as *"there is an art of getting rich."* It has 80,000 paying members at 3,000–4,000 yuan a year, about half an average monthly salary in Hangzhou. Members are urged to recruit others and climb named tiers. The hosts call it multi-level marketing, and she agrees. Its buyers: underdogs without elite degrees, small manufacturers who want AI for supply chains and Amazon listings, and vibe coders who want company.
6. **The "industrial slop complex."** Local governments, risk-averse and keen to show Beijing they back a strategic industry, back AI-generated video because it is the lowest bar for entering AI. Cities with little tech history are the keenest, Beijing's outlying Mentougou district among them. She cites a projection of 5 million AI short dramas next year. About 0.16% are hits and roughly 90% of AI-content companies lose money, *"ghost cities"* of content. Tracy Alloway links this to China's cycles of overcapacity; her example is pork after the swine-flu outbreak. Joe Weisenthal adds Brad Setser's view of those cycles.
7. **A low-trust, Japan-like mood.** Asked whether China is a high-trust society: *"No."* Since Covid, she says, it has turned zero-sum and *"dark forest"* (the phrase is from Liu Cixin's *Three-Body* novels). She says incomes have hardly risen in ten years, people avoid stocks and lost money on property, and 80–85% of high-speed rail lines lose money. She compares the mood to Japan's stalled economy: material comfort, few chances to grow. Astrology's revival after Covid is people seeking *"some interpretability in life."*
8. **Why China exports little culture.** Censorship is like *"walking in a dark room"* with ten pieces of furniture you must not touch. She also calls it a strategic choice: build factories and forge steel rather than a Hollywood. Weisenthal adds that the domestic market is large enough to make exports unnecessary.

## Debates and supersession

- **The optimism figure is not checked.** The description gives 84% against 38% from Stanford's AI Index; Wang says 83%. Neither the [[2026-04-30-ai-index-report-2026|AI Index 2026]] page nor the 2025 page records the China figure, so the number stays the episode's.
- **Optimism with low trust.** The episode holds two claims that pull apart. The public is more optimistic about AI than in the US, partly because people have no way to refuse it. And society has become low-trust and zero-sum since Covid. Wang offers both without reconciling them. The hosts contrast the second with the US, where low trust is the reason for pessimism.
- **One reporter's reporting.** The town, the education company and the drama industry come from her own visits and sources. The numbers (80,000 members, 0.16% hits, 90% losing money, 5 million dramas, 80–85% of rail lines) are given without sources in the episode.
