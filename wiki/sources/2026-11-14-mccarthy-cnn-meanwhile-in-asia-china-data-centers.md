# McCarthy (CNN, Meanwhile in Asia) — China's data-centre build-out and the US backlash

> The US midterm elections proved there's a growing backlash against AI and the infrastructure required to power it. But in China, data centers are going up - fast. CNN's Simone McCarthy tells Ivan Watson and Kunal Sehgal what it was like to visit a city in Inner Mongolia transformed by block upon block of hulking data centers - and discusses how China's fuelling their AI revolution.
>
> *— Channel description, CNN*

## TL;DR

A 22-minute episode of CNN's weekly *Meanwhile in Asia*, published 14 November 2026. Hosts **Ivan Watson** and **Kunal Sehgal** interview **Simone McCarthy**, CNN's senior China reporter, about her visit to **Ulanqab** in Inner Mongolia, *"China's potato growing capital"* turned data-centre hub, set against the US midterms, in which data centres became a campaign issue.

## Ulanqab (0:00–4:08)

The city is *"200 miles north of Beijing,"* grassland with sunflowers and wind turbines. McCarthy: *"there's about 2 million people in the region and they're slated to have 89 data centers built there… it's block after block of data centers. Construction workers are just lining the streets."* Huawei and Apple had data centres there before, *"but this is clearly this AI push."* Latency to Beijing, *"basically 2.1 milliseconds,"* makes it suitable (3:53).

Local business owners told her *"the economy has been bad in recent years"* and they were *"so happy for the extra business"* from construction workers, many of them migrants: *"double the business that they had a couple of years ago."* She notes the construction will end (11:15). [[2026-10-04-chu-liu-dw-the-dip-how-china-is-viewing-the-ai-race|Claire Chu on DW's The Dip]] names Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang and Gansu as the regions China routes training workloads to, and says local governments are equipped to address concerns *"before they can create real obstacles."*

## The US backlash (4:08–6:25)

The hosts' reading: it *"isn't necessarily opposition to AI itself, but it's what happens when this enormous technological revolution starts showing up in people's backyards,"* consuming electricity and water and pushing up bills, with noise and little consultation. Within two years it became *"a potent political issue,"* combined with *"suspicion about artificial intelligence in general."* They cite *"some $31 million"* in midterm ads about data centres (9:45).

McCarthy: people in China think more about *"how AI is going to be used practically,"* with *"more of an understanding that the government will keep AI in check,"* and without the US debate about *"the existential threat of humanity."* She links the missing backlash to that view and to limits on dissent: *"the space for public discussion in China and especially dissent in China is vastly different."* [[2026-07-29-ng-washington-post-china-open-source-ai-competitiveness|Andrew Ng on Washington Post Live]] discusses the same backlash: an adversary *"couldn't wish for almost anything better than these silly moratoriums on building our data centers,"* and he suspects the backlash is *"against AI as much as or even more than data centres per se."* See [[responsible-ai]] for the corpus's accounts of public pressure on AI.

## Scale, money and the national plan (5:43–8:19, 12:03–12:58)

The hosts put the US at *"more than 5,000"* data centres, about ten times China's *"approaching 500."* The [[2026-04-30-ai-index-report-2026|AI Index 2026]] counts 5,427 US data centres, more than ten times any other country. McCarthy says the count matters less than *"what's inside them,"* and attributes China's speed to permitting and to AI as a top national priority: embedding AI *"into manufacturing, into health care,"* moving *"way up the supply chain,"* and offsetting a declining population. *"There's 700 million AI users in China right now."* The hosts cite a **$560 billion plan announced in September** to scale up data infrastructure, without a source.

Placement is planned centrally: *"a national plan which has designated eight computing hubs and 10 so-called data center clusters"* (12:41). Her list of China's advantages: *"its ability to build out infrastructure, but also energy and centralized top-down planning."* See [[ai-sovereignty]].

## Chips: China's largest hurdle (8:19–12:03)

*"You can build as many physical data centers and racks as you want, but you need that high-tech hardware to fill it."* McCarthy names US limits on Chinese firms' access to the highest-end chips and China's own restrictions on importing *"not quite as high-end"* ones. With Democrats now controlling both houses of Congress, she reports concern in China about a push for more restrictions. Her unsourced figure: *"four of the US top tech firms have more computing power or more access to chips that they can use for AI than all of China's tech companies combined"* (9:32).

[[2026-09-18-reuters-on-assignment-inside-chinas-ai-race|Reuters' On Assignment]] covers the same balance: Eduardo Baptista says China *"can't really access even a fraction of the most advanced compute,"* that cheap power depends on the region (*"Texas to Inner Mongolia or Texas to Fujian"*), and that state-led expansion of generation *"could definitely help."*

## Energy, and East Data, West Compute (12:58–16:34)

The hosts cite the UN: data centres used about **448 TWh** in 2025, which as a country would rank 11th in electricity use. McCarthy: *"between 2022 and the end of 2025, China added the equivalent of the US's entire industrial power feeding into its grid,"* mostly wind, solar and hydro, alongside new coal plants that she says stabilise wind and solar. *"China wasted enough green energy in the first half of this year alone that would have been enough to power Mexico for an entire year."* The campus she toured gets *"upwards of 80% of their power directly from a wind farm,"* bypassing the grid.

Sehgal raises the catch: the energy is in the west and the demand in the east. China's 2022 strategy, *"eastern data, western compute,"* sited data centres where land is cheap and power plentiful, but for an autonomous vehicle or an AI-run factory *"you don't want a long delay in your transmission."* McCarthy: the strategy is *"undergoing some transformation. I wouldn't say it's been a complete failure… you can't do everything"* further away (16:20). [[2026-10-16-sivaram-shandal-bcg-pitching-progress-emerald-ai-flexibility|Varun Sivaram (Emerald AI)]] describes moving AI work between data centres when a local grid is stressed, treating inference as a range from latency-critical to batchable. See [[foundation-models]] for the corpus's power figures.

## Water (16:34–19:56)

The hosts cite the UN University's Institute for Water, Environment and Health: by 2030 data centres will use water equal to the needs of sub-Saharan Africa's 1.3 billion people; in 2025 their footprint was **4.5 trillion litres**. Ulanqab is on the edge of the Gobi, but cold most of the year. The campus she visited, run by **Envision**, cools with *"sort of an industrial alcohol instead of water."* Each building, *"off the top of my head… 300 megawatts,"* uses *"about a bathtub of water,"* mostly for bathrooms and kitchens: *"a zero water cooling system."* She cannot say what other data centres in the region do. An Envision executive calls the campus a *"pilot project"* to replicate in Latin America and South Africa, and McCarthy reports Chinese business people saying their technology *"could solve a lot of the problems in the US"* on energy but cannot be exported there.

## The closing frame (19:56–21:55)

A host: the US–China race is usually seen as one of *"technology companies and algorithms and the most advanced chips,"* but what could decide it is *"much more basic, which is land, electricity, water and the ability to actually build things."* McCarthy adds that the US still invests far more money.

For firms that build on, buy from or compete with either country's AI supply, the episode is a `contextual/external-triggers` source: it describes the external conditions (chip export controls, power and water, local politics, state planning) that set how much AI compute each country can bring online, and where.
