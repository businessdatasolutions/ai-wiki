# Romeo (Bloomberg New Economy Forum 2026) — we're watching the wrong AI race

> John Romeo, Managing Partner & CEO, Oliver Wyman Forum examines how humans can adapt and retain agency as AI becomes more powerful at the 2026 Bloomberg New Economy Forum in New Delhi.

## TL;DR

A 6:40 stage keynote on the **Bloomberg New Economy** channel, published 13 October 2026, by **John Romeo**, Managing Partner and CEO of the Oliver Wyman Forum, at the Bloomberg New Economy Forum in New Delhi. The argument is built on a single image, AlphaGo's **move 37** against Lee Sedol in 2016: a move commentators first took for a mistake, which *"overturned centuries of conventional wisdom."* Romeo's claim is that public attention is on one race, and two others matter as much:

1. **The race for intelligence** — the frontier labs, *"the US and China, hundreds of billions of dollars getting poured into chips, into models, into data centers."* Romeo thinks *"we're paying too much attention to that race."*
2. **The race for adaptation.** As AI becomes more capable and more accessible, *"the advantage shifts from the model to everything that surrounds the model: your data, your workflows, your operations, your processes."* There are pilots everywhere and value is scarce *"except perhaps in the bottom line,"* because *"AI creates time. It creates capacity, but it doesn't automatically create value."* More often than not the bottleneck just moves.
3. **The race for control.** *"Give an agent your data, a budget, a mandate, and permission to act, and you've created an economic actor."* When the agent choosing an airline is not a human, *"the battle that we fought for human attention shifts to a battle for machine permission, and power follows permission."*

## Key claims

### The adaptation race: only 8% have a standard for AI-saved time

The one statistic of the talk comes from new Oliver Wyman Forum research: **only 8% of organisations have an organisation-wide standard for what happens to the time AI saves.** The slide (see Visual canon) shows the other side, 92% without one, and draws the conclusion that *"a great deal of the productivity AI creates is still being left on the table."* No sample, method or date is given.

The worked example: *"An insurance claim can be processed in seconds. But if that insurance claim then sits in a queue for three days waiting for approval, you've just made one step spectacularly efficient. You haven't transformed a company."* And the general form: *"The faster the machine gets, the more visible the rest of the organization becomes… a million automated processes don't create a transformed company."* The firms creating value *"are changing how they operate."*

### The control race: license plates for agents

As AI grows more capable, *"the question shifts from what can AI do to what are we prepared to let it do."* Romeo relays a suggestion from **Tim Berners-Lee**: *"give agents license plates. So if an agent does harm, we know whose it was, who's accountable, what it was allowed to do and who can stop it."* He reduces it to four words: **identity, permission, accountability, revocability.**

He pairs it with an observation from **Yuval Noah Harari**: people lie and cheat, which is why we created courts, contracts, auditors and regulators; *"civilization works not because we trust everyone, but because we've learned to cooperate with those that we can't necessarily completely trust. And the same is true for AI."* The institutions needed are not ones that make machines blindly trustworthy but ones that *"allow us to work with machines that explicitly we don't trust."*

The paradox he draws from this: *"increasingly we're going to need machines that supervise machines."* Humans do not leave; they *"have to go up a level,"* because the better the boundaries and the revocability, the more can be delegated. His summary: **"machine speed oversight, but ultimately a human accountability."**

### Sovereignty as preserving choice

The same challenge applies to countries. Oliver Wyman Forum research in India finds strong demand both for building AI as a national capability and for access to the world's best models. Romeo reads this as *"a new definition of sovereignty that focuses on emphasizing the permission and preserving choice rather than eliminating dependence."* Countries need not regulate AI identically: *"We just need interoperable accountability."*

### Not spectators

Romeo turns against his own framing: it *"makes us sound like spectators, like the only choice that we really have is how quickly we adapt."* Employees feel this way; **68% say they would take a pay cut for a guarantee that AI would never replace them** (Oliver Wyman Forum, per the slide). His answer: *"Technology has a direction because people give it one."* Companies decide what machines may do, governments set the rules, and society should have a voice in what the technology is for. He closes on move 37 again: welcome it in medicine and materials science, but *"what happens when move 37 comes to a financial market, to a payment system, to a military decision?"* People must *"write the rules for the games, and indeed determine which games the machines are even allowed to play."*

## Dynamic-capabilities reading

- **`digital-transforming/redesigning-internal-structures`** — the adaptation race is a workflow-redesign argument: value goes to firms that are *"reimagining their workflows"* and *"changing how they operate,"* and the missing organisation-wide standard for AI-saved time is a gap in internal structure, not in the technology.
- **`contextual/external-triggers`** — agents acting as economic actors (choosing airlines, holding budgets) are an external shift that changes whose attention a firm must win; Romeo calls it the move from human attention to machine permission.
