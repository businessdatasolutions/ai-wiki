# Keneally (BCG, The So What) — LLM or MD: who owns the patient journey?

> Ben Keneally, BCG's Asia Pacific health care services leader, explains why AI is now many patients' first stop — often before they see a doctor. He argues that pairing AI with personal health records improves outcomes and equity, and that health leaders, not LLMs, must shape how this shift unfolds.
>
> You'll Learn: Why AI is becoming the default first step in care, especially in markets with limited access to clinicians. How pairing AI with personal health records improves treatment compliance and catches errors earlier. Why driving AI into end-to-end care pathways takes leadership, not just frontline experimentation.

## TL;DR

A 21-minute episode of *The So What from BCG* on the [[Boston Consulting Group]] channel, published 14 July 2026. Host **Georgie Frost** interviews **Ben Keneally**, managing director and partner and BCG's Asia Pacific leader for health care services. It accompanies BCG's report *Consumers Are Ready for AI-Enabled Health Care. Health Systems Need to Be, Too.* (linked, not ingested). Five claims:

1. **AI is the first step in care.** *"The biggest, fastest change we've seen in hundreds of years in how people access health care."* People use LLMs for assurance and to decide the next step, *"a step on a pathway to treatment."*
2. **Adoption is highest in developing nations.** In a BCG survey of about 15 countries and thousands of internet-connected people, developing nations led, because free helplines and clinics are scarcer there. Within developed countries the wealthier use AI more; migrants with language barriers are a prominent use case.
3. **From chat to agents.** Next is an *"always-on health agent"* connected to wearables and records that advises on diet, sleep and exercise, books appointments and prompts immunisations, which raises open questions on who provides it (national systems or private firms) and how it is licensed and assured.
4. **Pair the model with the person's own records.** Patients with access to their records catch errors in their treatment, comply better with it and know what to do when symptoms change; an LLM that explains the records adds context, *"and we know that that leads to … substantially better health outcomes and, in fact, more equitable health outcomes."*
5. **Health systems should shape it, not stop it.** Two routes, *"both/and"*: influence the public models (*"how do I make my information, my best practice guidelines … available to those models"*) and bring LLMs behind the firewall, weighted to local guidelines and population. The unlock is leadership: frontline experiments need no one else's agreement, while an end-to-end pathway needs everyone on board.

## Key claims

### Risk, in proportion

Asked about dangerous advice: *"it happens from humans just as much as it happens from technology."* He cites an average of 18 years for best practice to move from the lab to most patients, a gap AI could compress. Commercial providers have *"an incentive not to end up getting sued,"* and regulators want good advice too, so he expects the risk to be manageable; the larger risk is settling for symptom checkers when *"the opportunity here is so much bigger."*

### Who owns the patient relationship

It is *"a critical strategic question,"* and it is not yet clear how consumer AI will make money: subscription, or free versions with advertising. Either way, providers and payers need a strong consumer experience and must make sure that when an LLM advises, it draws on their advice and reputation.

### AI plus clinicians

AI can do supervised jobs on behalf of clinicians, such as applying simple algorithms to test results, but *"health is a human-to-human contact service."* The aim is to use AI to remove administration and gatekeeping (for example, routine three-monthly check-ins for chronic disease) so that clinicians' time goes into the relationship. Workforce shortages make this a necessity rather than an option.
