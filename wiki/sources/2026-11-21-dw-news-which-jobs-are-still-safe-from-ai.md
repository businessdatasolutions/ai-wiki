# Schmidt (DW News) — which jobs are still safe from AI

> AI and AI-powered robots are advancing rapidly. Entry-level jobs are already disappearing in offices. Which jobs are truly secure, and who might be replaced next?
>
> *— Channel description, DW News (hashtags and links omitted)*

## TL;DR

An eight-minute television report on [[DW News]], published 21 November 2026, by Miltiades Schmidt for DW's business desk. It puts the title question to **three apprentices in Germany**: an information electronics technician, a nursing student and an office administration apprentice. Their employers and an AI expert, **Laura Tönnsen** (ARIC Institute), answer it for each occupation, and footage of a machine already doing part of each job is set against it.

1. **Repetition is the risk factor.** Tönnsen: *"If you have jobs that involve essentially the same tasks over and over again, they're naturally more at risk than if you have a very varied workflow."*
2. **The trade looks safe, for now.** The electrical firm's managing director expects a robot for *"laying pipes, painting, and things like that"* at some point, but *"when it comes to troubleshooting and more complex cases, you still need a human being."* The report shows a robot already inspecting control cabinets in China.
3. **Nursing looks safest.** The interpersonal side and an ageing population protect it, and applications for nursing training have risen, including from *"very young men."* A robot in Taiwan carries samples, and an AI assistant in Germany takes over documentation.
4. **The office apprenticeship is the one at risk, and its employer says so.** *"We won't need as many workers as we did before, at least in the office… We won't be doing much training in that format anymore."*
5. **The aggregate view is mild, the job-title view is not.** Tönnsen: earlier studies suggest new jobs will *"balance things out somewhat,"* but *"in the coming years, we really won't have certain job titles anymore."*
6. **The policy close:** retraining office workers for AI-proof jobs *"seems appropriate,"* and governments are *"still waiting to see how to handle the situation."*

A news report built on six interviews and vendor footage. It gives no figures of its own beyond one operator's claim; see [Source quality](#source-quality).

## Repetitive work and three apprentices (0:00–0:55)

The report opens with Tönnsen's test, repetitive against varied work, and introduces the three apprentices through soundbites: *"AI can't do hands-on work,"* *"AI will never be able to do this job,"* *"These tasks need to be done by people."* An intro graphic pairs each with a machine (still 1 in the [Visual canon](#visual-canon)). Tönnsen adds: *"People's fears should always be taken seriously, especially now given the rapid pace of technological development."*

## The trade: electricians, and a robot in China (0:55–2:08)

Walid Altaleb, an apprentice information electronics technician, says AI *"was a minor consideration"* in his choice: *"it was clear to me that I didn't want anything office related, but rather a trade."* His managing director, Sascha Fynn Kitschke (Bergmann Electrical and Antenna Technology): *"You don't see any robots here yet. I think it'll be a long time before we do see them in this field. That makes it very AI proof."* He then draws the line inside the job between routine installation, which a robot will do *"at some point,"* and troubleshooting, which needs a person.

The narration answers with China, which is *"even further along. A robot is already inspecting control cabinets there,"* and with robot hands of growing dexterity: *"Will the AI electrician arrive sooner than expected?"* The footage credits the robot to FALCONIX. [[2026-07-30-hines-pierce-mckinsey-ai-physical-world-more-valuable|Hines-Pierce on the McKinsey podcast]] describes the same occupation from the supply side: *"We're not producing enough electricians,"* and *"it's just going to show up in increased costs."*

## The office, part one: entry-level work in the US and at consulting firms (2:08–2:51)

The narration: in the US *"the number of jobs for entry-level workers is shrinking, possibly as a result of AI which is taking over more and more routine tasks."* It names no source; the corpus's payroll evidence on that claim is [[2026-04-28-brynjolfsson-canaries-coal-mine|Canaries in the Coal Mine]] and its successors (see [[ai-employment-effects]]). Tönnsen, over footage of OpenAI's ChatGPT Record and Amazon's Rufus: *"It can now do more than just create call logs or handle customer service… we've also heard reports that this is leading to fewer entry-level positions. For example, at large consulting firms in particular, you're very busy at the beginning doing research and preparing presentations. These are areas where AI has already come a long way."* [[2026-09-10-sundararajan-wef-radio-davos-thrive-in-age-of-ai|Sundararajan on Radio Davos]] describes the same junior work (*"PowerPoint, spreadsheets, routine coding"*) as one side of an apprenticeship bargain that generative AI is fragmenting.

## Nursing: the relationship, more applicants, a robot and documentation (2:51–4:38)

Josefine Hausmann, a nursing student, already cares for patients on her own. The narration credits *"the interpersonal aspect"* with protecting her; she wanted *"a job that AI can't take away,"* and *"when people express their gratitude to me, a little light comes on inside."* The desire for a secure job *"has led to a recent increase in the number of applicants for nursing training,"* and Teresa Maria Buchholz, Head of Training at Schön Klinik Hamburg Eilbek, notes *"very young men applying."* No figure is given for either.

Two machines follow. In Taiwan, **Nurabot** transports samples; *"according to its operator,"* it *"saves its human colleague two or three hours of work per day,"* which *"results in potential savings on staff, even though they are primarily nursing assistants."* The footage is credited to [[NVIDIA]], and the intro graphic captions it *Taichung Veterans General Hospital*. In Germany, *"a digital AI assistant has recently begun to enter patient data, a task that was once very time-consuming,"* shown as an application drafting a discharge letter (still 3). Hausmann: *"I think it's great that we can now use the voice feature to work with AI. Even just for documentation."* The narration leaves its own question open: *"Will the demand for nurses eventually sink due to AI?"* See [[ai-in-health-care]].

## Job losses in Germany, and job titles that disappear (4:38–5:23)

The narration expects new jobs too, *"such as consultants who specialize in AI applications."* Tönnsen: *"Previous studies often suggested that there will be job losses, but at the same time, new job profiles and new jobs will emerge and the overall effect will actually balance things out somewhat. So, we'll only notice minor effects there. But I do believe that in the coming years, we really won't have certain job titles anymore. They will disappear."* She names no study.

## The office apprentice and her employer (5:23–6:32)

Tetiana Podoroha fled Ukraine and found an apprenticeship as an office administrator after many rejections: *"I realized that my German isn't very good, but I'd still like to improve it. Now I'm happy."* Her employer is the same electrical firm. The narration: AI *"will take over many of her tasks, such as coordinating schedules."* Kitschke states the competitive logic and its consequence for training:

> *"If other companies pull ahead of us and let's say make greater use of AI, then we have a problem here. And that's why I think we won't need as many workers as we did before, at least in the office. So, we'll just have to figure out how to distribute this now. We won't be doing much training in that format anymore. Let's just say that needs to be stated very clearly."*

The same firm thus appears twice: its trade apprentice is described as AI-proof, and its office apprenticeship as a format it will largely stop offering. [[2026-10-01-dw-news-estonia-young-people-entry-level-jobs|DW News's Estonia report]] credits Germany's and the Netherlands' dual apprenticeship systems with their low youth unemployment; this report shows one employer inside that system deciding which apprenticeships to keep. [[2026-10-17-dw-shift-how-ai-is-changing-the-working-world|DW Shift's report]] covers the entry-level squeeze in Germany from the graduate side.

## Graduates, STEM, and the closing (6:32–8:08)

*"In this age of AI, is it even worth going to college anymore?"* Tönnsen recommends STEM degrees, *"anything related to mathematics, engineering, the natural sciences, and technology,"* and wants Germany to attract young professionals to them. The narration's summary: the electrician trade *"should remain secure for a long time, not least because every construction site is different,"* since robots *"can only get around in spaces in which they've been trained."* Nursing *"seems to be even more secure. Demand is growing worldwide as populations age."* Buchholz: *"When it comes to interacting with the patient directly, I don't think AI can really be of much help because that also involves the emotional connection we build with the patient."* [[2026-10-07-richardson-reuters-econ-world-ai-at-work|Richardson (ADP) on Reuters Econ World]] reports the US counterpart in payroll data: early-career home health aides gaining while exposed occupations decline, and ageing, not AI, as the larger labour-market force.

The close: *"AI threatens to cause massive job losses. It seems appropriate to retrain office workers for AI proof jobs. We can't yet accurately assess the extent of upheaval that AI will cause. That's why governments around the world are still waiting to see how to handle the situation."* [[2026-11-19-raimondo-holcomb-harvard-iop-managing-unemployment-ai-economy|Raimondo and Holcomb at the Harvard IOP]] discuss the same gap from the US side: Raimondo has *"almost no confidence that the federal government will do anything until there's a massive crisis,"* and Raise US asks large employers to let people *"get paid full-time, but spend half their time being retrained."*
