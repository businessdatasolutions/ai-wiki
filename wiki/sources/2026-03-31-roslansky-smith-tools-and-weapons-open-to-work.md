# Roslansky & Smith (Tools and Weapons) — turning AI anxiety into skills

> In this episode of Tools and Weapons, Microsoft's Vice Chair and President Brad Smith sits down with Ryan Roslansky, CEO of LinkedIn and Executive Vice President at Microsoft, for a practical conversation about AI, the future of work, and what people can do now to stay ahead. Drawing on ideas from his new book, "Open to Work: How to Get Ahead in the Age of AI," Ryan shares a framework for thinking about careers in the era of AI that starts not with job titles, but with tasks.

## TL;DR

A 41-minute episode of *Tools and Weapons*, Brad Smith's podcast on the [[Microsoft]] channel, published 31 March 2026. Smith, Microsoft's vice chair and president, interviews **Ryan Roslansky**, CEO of LinkedIn, about *Open to Work*, the book Roslansky wrote with **Aneesh Raman**. Five claims:

1. **Jobs are tasks, not titles**, in three buckets: tasks AI will automate (*"Summarize this document"*), tasks it augments (*"a superpower"*), and tasks *"so innately human or messy"* that AI will not do them, such as getting a disagreeing room to agree.
2. **The human skills that were called soft are the hard currency now:** *"curiosity, courage, communication, compassion."*
3. **Skills change fast regardless of AI.** On LinkedIn's data, the skills required for the average job changed by 25% over the past eight years, and LinkedIn expects 70% change by 2030. *"Even if you're not changing your job, your job is changing on you."*
4. **One builder instead of four roles.** LinkedIn created a **builder** role that combines product manager, product marketer, designer and front-end engineer, plus an **associate builder** entry route for people *"coming out of college, maybe not even going to college at all."* Product managers now write evals rather than specs.
5. **A schism in the industry.** Smith names *"the emerging great debate"*: AI built to *"outperform humans at… most economically valuable work"* (AGI) against AI built to *"empower people to do more."*

## Key claims

### A framework, not a forecast

Roslansky frames the book as *"not as a crystal ball… but more… as a framework"* for turning uncertainty into opportunity, and *"the future isn't written."* Smith supports the modesty with an anecdote: he asked Copilot's Researcher agent to grade well-known technologists' past predictions, and *"almost everybody got between 20 and 30%."*

### Three task buckets, and using the middle one

Classify your own day by bucket and ask what share of your job is in each. Roslansky's bucket-two examples from his own work: Copilot in Outlook summarising a long email debate (*"I can save a good ten to 15 minutes on a topic"*), and asking it to challenge an important email before he sends it, *"as a thought partner."* Moving tasks from bucket one to bucket two frees time for bucket three, which Smith calls *"where we're often the most time constrained,"* such as a manager asking colleagues how they are doing.

### Careers as climbing walls

People ask LinkedIn for career paths, but *"there is no such thing as a linear career path."* Think less about the next title and more about *"what adjacent skills or tasks can I be picking up."* He sees individual contributors who once assumed they had to become managers staying ICs, because with AI they can do more. The book's three questions: why do you work, what do you uniquely do, and where do you want to go.

### How jobs are changing

Software engineering is changing, not going away: LinkedIn sees *"an increase in the number of software engineer postings."* A product manager still talks to customers, but the next step is now *"writing a bunch of evals for the model"*: *"If this scenario, here's a good or bad response."* At LinkedIn, siloed roles in software production (PM, product marketer, designer, front-end engineer) required a communication path between them; one builder with AI tools can take a product from start to finish, so the company can test more. *"It doesn't mean anyone's role is going away. We're making more builders."*

### Outperform or empower

Smith: some in the industry pursue AGI defined as a system that outperforms humans at most economically valuable work; Microsoft's mission is *"technology that empowers people to do more."* Roslansky's answer stays with the second: AI as *"an enabler for us to do bigger and better things as a society,"* which needs governments, schools and companies as well as technology firms.
