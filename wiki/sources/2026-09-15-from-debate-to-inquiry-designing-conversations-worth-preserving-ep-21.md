# From Debate to Inquiry: Designing Conversations Worth Preserving | Ep. 21

> In Episode 21 of the MIT Sloan CIO Podcast, Allan Tate and Irving Wladawsky-Berger continue the Collective Sensemaking series with a look at how leaders can design conversations that lead to learning rather than simply reinforce familiar positions. (Part 3 of 4)
>
> They explore the evolution of the Inquiry Framework, a structured approach to stabilizing the question at hand, considering multiple defensible perspectives, testing them against evidence and real-world use cases, and making areas of tension visible without forcing consensus too early.
>
> The conversation also examines the difference between structured uncertainty and indecision. For CIOs and other leaders, inquiry does not replace the need to make decisions. Instead, it can create a stronger foundation for judgment by clarifying assumptions, alternatives, and available evidence.
>
> Listen to Part 3 of this four-part Collective Sensemaking series to hear how better question design can make disagreement more productive and turn important conversations into lasting organizational learning.
>
> — channel description, MIT Sloan CIO Symposium Videos (excerpt; event and community notices omitted)

## TL;DR

A ~73-minute episode of the [[MIT Sloan CIO Symposium]]'s podcast, published 15 September 2026 and recorded on 2 September. Part 3 of the Collective Sensemaking series with [[Allan Tate]] and [[Irving Wladawsky-Berger]]. [[2026-09-02-from-conversation-to-institutional-understanding-ep-20|Episode 20]] left a question open: since not every conversation produces learning, what makes one worth preserving? This episode's answer is the **Inquiry Framework**, which Tate says he *"primarily developed"* and the two have practised for almost a year. Tate opens by settling the series length: *"we started the series thinking it was going to be a three-part series, but in fact, it's going to be a four-part series"* [0:00]. He also says the framework came first and its fit with collective sensemaking was *"serendipity"* [4:04].

**1. Inquiry, not debate (4:04–12:02).** Wladawsky-Berger's model is the research community: a conference or a journal issue carries many points of view because the topic is new. Tate's version is that this is not an academic framework moved into industry but one *"specifically designed uh to be useful to highlevel executives"* [6:00]. He brought debates to the symposium pre-event around 2024, from his work with MIT students. Three reasons it did not suit executives [9:00–11:02]:
- A debate starts from a yes-or-no resolution; inquiry wants a richer question explored through **lenses**.
- A debate looks for the answer; inquiry focuses on the question.
- Poll an audience before and after a debate and *"only a fraction"* change their mind, so they leave still facing uncertainty and multiple opinions. Inquiry starts from that.

**2. Origin: the AI-consciousness conversation (12:02–19:03).** Wladawsky-Berger was considering a blog post on AI consciousness; Tate advised against making it a focus, calling much media coverage *"clickbait"* built on undefined terms. Tate then worked out the framework over many iterations with his AI assistant. Wladawsky-Berger: *"I don't think it was a disagreement. I think it was an exploration."* His reason to drop the topic: *"what the hell do we know about consciousness?"* That is for philosophers and neuroscientists, and for a CIO audience *"AI consciousness is not relevant. What are they going to do with that?"* [16:01–17:01]. He contrasts AI in software engineering, a subject the hosts know and the audience needs.

**3. Designing the question (19:03–30:02).** The first inquiry series was planned as seven parts. Its first question went through rewrites. An early draft, *"can machines solve novel problems without explicit human instruction"*, was *"riddled with uh places for people to get tripped up. You know, what do you mean by novel? What do you mean by human instruction?"* The used version: *"how do machines approach problems they have not previously encountered?"* [20:03–21:01, 30:02]. Rules for a question: its words should be self-evident, it should not be yes-or-no, and it should be stable. Each question gets three lenses, *"none of which would be 100% right or wrong"*. For the first question these were: *applies learned patterns*, *exhibits behaviors not explicitly anticipated*, and *frames and solves problems autonomously*. The shift is from which side is right to which interpretation best fits the evidence. Wladawsky-Berger adds that concrete cases make the lenses usable: software developers, customer-service staff and researchers give different evidence. He also compares this to prompting: the clearer you are about what you are after, the better a model, or an audience, can help.

**4. Running an inquiry (24:01–29:02).** The hosts own the question and the lenses. Three lenses is a convention, not *"a magic number"*. An audience member can propose a fourth, and lenses can each hold *"a partial truth"*. A session runs: question, lenses, a concrete use case for each, the **tensions**, and finally why it matters to a CIO, who must decide *"in the face of uncertainty"*. A volunteer who ran the 2026 pre-event questions adapted the format slightly, which Tate counts as fine.

**5. The layer cake (29:02–34:00).** The seven-part series was built in tiers: **fundamental** (what machines can do; four questions), **applied** (how machine intelligence should be evaluated), **advanced** (agency, meaning, responsibility; relevant to agentic AI) and an unused **speculative** tier, where questions such as machine experience, and consciousness, would sit. Wladawsky-Berger's condition for any speculative question: a CIO or MIT graduate student must know what to do with the answer. Both agree the order matters.

**6. Structured inquiry versus indecision (39:00–43:00).** Tate's question: what separates structured inquiry from indecision? Wladawsky-Berger: evidence and use cases; a claim without a concrete case is hard to use. Tate's summary: the framework explores options, gives evidence and names the tensions *"so that the chief… information officer rather can uh take that exploration and make judgments"* [42:00–43:00]. In the closing he adds: *"this isn't to say that we want them to evade questions… We do want them to make decisions. We just want them to make informed decisions"* [1:06:00].

**7. A worked lens: unanticipated behaviour (43:00–50:02).** The framework was piloted on the podcast, in an episode on how historians will view our time (Tate thinks episode 13), and then the seven-part series. For the lens *exhibits behaviors not explicitly anticipated*, Tate's example: while preparing this episode, the model colour-coded points in red for the hosts' attention because it had seen Tate do so before. He reads it as newer models being allowed, within safety limits, to infer what a user wants when the step is reversible. He names three layers behind such behaviour: pre-training, the later training for safety and manner, and project context supplied to an instance, with saved state and memories carried from one instance to the next.

**8. Collective sensemaking needs an architecture (50:02–1:03:00).** Tate draws the line for episode 22: collective sensemaking is *"the discovery that the machine can connect the dots across conversations"*; doing it at scale is the **executive learning architecture**, which needs saved and indexed memory records, state handed between threads, and *"garbage collection"* [50:02–51:02]. It is *"an internal tool"* not yet exposed to the community. The report is a point in time; the records form *"a traceable record"* of how views change. Wladawsky-Berger compares it to AI in programming: developers offload what they would have to hold in their heads and keep their judgement for what only they can do. Tate takes the parallel further: task specifications are *"a highlevel programming language"* for a repeatable process. He then ties the two halves together. The Inquiry Framework *"gives people license to change their minds more easily"*, and the architecture *"reduces the cost of an organization changing direction"* because the reasons for decisions survive staff turnover [58:03–59:03]. Whether this scales to an enterprise *"remains to be seen"*.

**9. Close (1:04:01–1:13:19).** Tate reports that his wife said hearing about the work made her *"less afraid"* of AI, which he puts down to its augmenting a human collaboration rather than replacing a task. His summary: inquiry is an alternative to debate, not a replacement; it puts questions before answers; three lenses keep it usable; the framework is *"an evolving experiment"*, not doctrine; and *"the humans remain responsible for framing participation interpretation and action"*. Any one conversation is a moment in time: accumulating insight needs memory, provenance, operating principles, governance and human review, the subject of [[2026-10-07-from-conversations-to-an-executive-learning-architecture-ep-22|episode 22]]. Asked what more is needed to turn dialogue into sustained learning, Wladawsky-Berger says he does not know yet: *"I want to see the evidence and I want to see the use case."*
