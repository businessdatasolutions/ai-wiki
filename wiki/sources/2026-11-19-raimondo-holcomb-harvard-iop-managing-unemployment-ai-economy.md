## Raise US: governors, philanthropy and pilots (2:35–10:10)

The introducing student says Raise US *"launched with more than $500 million committed to new education and workforce training."* Raimondo, five months in: *"We've raised three-quarters of a billion dollars."* Holcomb describes the phase as moving *"from pitching to now being very picky"*: *"if you've got a good idea on the left or a good idea on the right we want to learn from it and test it and then scale it. So, we're in the execution phase."*

Raimondo on why states: *"we chose to work with governors… also because I have almost no confidence that the federal government will do anything until there's a massive crisis."* Two pilots:

- **A year of service.** *"We think the AI transition could be tough for entry-level workers… nobody wants to hire you if you don't have work experience. How do you get work experience?"* With Maryland's Governor Moore, and next in Colorado, Raise US funds a year in jobs that are open: *"healthcare jobs, behavioral health care… teaching, literacy coaches."* The hope is that people *"not only learn skills, but get a job at the end."* The student moderator also names partnerships in Arkansas and Connecticut.
- **Hospital billing staff in Utah.** At the state's largest employer, the Intermountain hospital system, Raimondo expects revenue-cycle work, *"basically fighting with insurance companies to get paid,"* to go to software: *"eventually the hospital's agents are going to be fighting with insurance companies' agents and all those people, mostly women, mostly non-college-educated women I fear, are going to be out of a job."* Instead of waiting, the project identifies these staff now and retrains them for clinical openings, *"radiology technician, operating room technician and the like."* See [[ai-in-health-care]], where administration and billing come first among hospital AI uses in the Dutch and US surveys.

Her summary: *"our thesis is we want to run a lot of experiments, see what works, try policy changes, use philanthropy to spur the change because… if all we do is have a tech strategy and move forward with AI and we wind up with 10, 20% unemployment, that's very bad for America."*

[[2026-07-22-brown-wef-meet-the-leader-entry-level-jobs-in-an-ai-era|Peter Brown of PwC on WEF's Meet the Leader]] asks the same question about entry-level experience from the firm's side: how to give juniors the experience that used to come from doing the tasks AI now does.

## The unemployment rate and what it hides (10:10–15:32)

Deming frames the uncertainty. Amodei has predicted that *"half of all entry-level white-collar jobs"* will be gone *"by 2027 or 2028 or whatever it was."* Yet *"the unemployment rate is 4.1%. And the unemployment rate for new college graduates is 5.2%… those numbers are actually historically low. So we went through an entire decade in the 2010s when the unemployment rate was higher than 4.1%."* He asks whether a crisis that may come next year or in five years calls for different strategies.

Holcomb: *"one report doesn't make a whole story… Don't misinterpret full employment… and say everything's fine."* He points to underemployment and *"the K-shaped economy"*, and to converting *"more have-nots into haves"*: *"if we're going to as a nation win this so-called AI race, Americans… need to be the winners."* Raimondo adds the numbers in the TL;DR, and that *"entry-level hiring, presently, it's starting to get challenging."* The programmes Raise US funds, *"outcome-based funding of higher ed, which we're going to be piloting in Indiana… apprenticeships, years of service, wage insurance,"* are things *"we should already have. We're hoping to use the occasion of AI to motivate the change that's necessary."* Her conclusion: *"the unemployment rate like hugely masks the actual picture."*

Holcomb on scale: people working two or three jobs *"drive past a career opportunity every day"* that could *"double or triple their salary if they had the skill set"*; and *"the federal government investing 800 billion every year into workforce development projects"* should *"start funding what works."* See [[ai-employment-effects]] for the corpus's measurements of the entry-level and aggregate effects.

## Congress, the midterms and pilots in the states (15:32–19:46)

Raimondo: *"these days Congress tends not to act on anything big until we're already in full-blown crisis."* Her theory: *"most companies really haven't implemented AI… they're in like the first inning… So the fact that yes, we're seeing some folks being let go, some reduction in… entry-level hiring, that doesn't surprise me at all. I would be very surprised if two years from now we aren't seeing some pretty serious upticks in unemployment because by then they'll be like really rewiring their systems."* (See [[enterprise-ai-adoption]] on breadth against depth of adoption.)

The governors' pilots are meant for that moment: *"what would happen if we had a… tax break for payroll taxes if people hire young workers… if the federal government put as much money into effective workforce programs as they do into less effective ones,"* so that Congress *"might look around the country and say, 'Okay, this pilot worked.'"* On evidence: *"although we're at Harvard and I know there's a lot of folks here who value like randomized control trials, as do I, the governor and I know what really matters in getting things done is having a coalition of political support behind your ideas and good data to show they work."* The coalition: *"probably 30 companies, lots of governors, lots of philanthropy."*

Holcomb reads the electorate as *"in a pretty transactional mood… looking for results,"* and the economy as *"AI-infused… plus quantum plus robots and humanoids."* Working with up to 50 governors means *"a lot of different test cases in different verticals."*

## What each party gives up: permitting and the CHIPS Act (19:46–27:28)

Deming asks what each party should concede to put good jobs first. Raimondo's answer is the CHIPS Act. After the money was voted, *"I was told that it would take seven years before TSMC… could put a shovel in the ground"* because of local, state and federal permitting and NEPA. The White House would not help because *"they didn't want to upset the environmentalists,"* so she worked with Republican senator Ted Cruz and Democrat Mark Kelly on a bipartisan law exempting the projects from NEPA *"because they were critical to national security. Today, for the first time ever in America's history, TSMC is building leading edge semiconductors, employing thousands of people, have started producing their second factory, and on their way to four more."* See [[ai-sovereignty]] for the corpus's accounts of domestic chip and compute capacity.

She adds reopening Rhode Island's schools in autumn 2020 against the teachers' unions and her pension reform against the public-sector unions: *"None of these problems are hard to fix in like the substance of them. It's the politics that makes them hard."* Democrats, *"the party of government,"* have *"a responsibility to make it work, but that means calling it out when it doesn't work"*; *"less red tape, less permitting, building, all that would create good jobs."* Holcomb, to his own party: *"don't let the perfect be the enemy of the good. Don't subscribe to purity tests,"* adding SK Hynix to her examples and recalling how closely Indiana worked with her department, despite grief from his own side for *"reaching out to the other party."*

## Data centres and public fear (27:28–32:20)

Morgan Jay asks whether Americans' fear of data centres is justified. Holcomb: *"knowledge is power, compute is power, and it's part of our arsenal as a country… if we say enough is enough… that's retreating."* Indiana mayors who accept data centres are *"addressing the water concerns… the noise concerns… the electricity concerns and they're negotiating deals that have a huge upside if done right."* His advice: *"start locally and… be very transparent… some communities don't want it, okay? Some do and let's work with the ones that do."*

Raimondo: *"I think there were 45 or more ballot initiatives which were a form of moratorium on data centers and all but one passed… AI played a big role in the election mostly because everybody's afraid… and so when you're afraid you want to stop it, which is I don't believe a good thing."* At a meeting in a church in Danville, Indiana, people asked whether they or their children would have jobs after paying for college; *"one young man was joking, 'I'm picking out my post-AI job. Maybe I'll be a gardener.'"* Others did not want *"to be left behind"*: a small-business owner who *"can't afford to hire more employees,"* a man whose prescriptions are now ready when he arrives. *"People are legitimately afraid. We cannot blow that off. They're afraid of their jobs… their electricity… their kids' mental health, cyber hacks."* To Silicon Valley's case against regulation: *"if you don't regulate and you become reckless you will have regulation and it will stifle innovation."* See [[responsible-ai]].

[[2026-11-14-mccarthy-cnn-meanwhile-in-asia-china-data-centers|CNN's Meanwhile in Asia]], the same week, reports the midterm side in figures (about $31 million in ads about data centres) and the hosts' reading that the backlash combines local costs with *"suspicion about artificial intelligence in general."* [[2026-07-29-ng-washington-post-china-open-source-ai-competitiveness|Andrew Ng on Washington Post Live]] calls the moratoriums *"silly"* and a gift to an adversary, and puts the challenge in helping everyone gain new skills rather than in a job apocalypse.

## What Raise US asks of employers (32:20–39:17)

Raimondo: *"most of our work is working with at this point large employers"* whose call-centre, warehouse or revenue-cycle staff may be replaced. Employers are *"pretty honest"*: *"once the AI gets to be able to do this and we've integrated it, these thousand jobs might go to a 100 jobs."* The challenge she puts to them: *"if you think you're going to go from a thousand jobs to a 100 jobs in the next 18 months, let's get to work now with these folks… Let people get paid full-time, but spend half their time being retrained. Consider redeploying these people in your own organization. Consider slowing the rate of firing to provide time for the economy to absorb this."* Her model: *"This is an agentic economy. There'll be new jobs created of a different kind in different industries. What we are very stressed about is jobs going away before the new ones get created."* Hence safety-net work *"especially for older workers."* Raise US works less on how AI companies build models than on *"how it's being implemented to cushion the blow."* And on incentives: *"If you tell companies, 'Fire everybody and don't sweat it, and it'll improve your stock price,' they're going to fire everybody… So I think we have to come up with some of these incentives."*

[[2026-06-03-falk-tsoukalas-the-ai-layoff-trap|Falk and Tsoukalas]] model that incentive formally: competing firms automate past the collectively optimal rate because each bears only part of the demand it destroys. They find voluntary agreements among firms not self-enforcing, retraining a partial remedy, and a per-task automation tax the only full one.

Holcomb: *"it has to be business-led… the pressure to compete against business in China or anywhere else in the world that's deploying… this new technology."* He recasts the public-private partnership as a *"P5"*: the private sector first, with government at every level and post-secondary education supporting, philanthropy complementing, and the individual citizen with *"their responsibility… in this grand bargain."* His example is Cook Group, an Indiana medical-device maker: *"because of AI and robots overseas, it's… harder and harder to compete,"* so *"they have created their own university in their company to teach AI not just literacy but fluency and mastery and not find themselves in a position of laying off but find themselves growing as a company."*

Two W&W cells apply to this employer side. **`contextual/external-triggers`**: Holcomb places the pressure to reskill in foreign competitors using AI and robots, the disruptive-competitor trigger. **`digital-transforming/improving-digital-maturity`**: Raimondo's asks (retraining on paid time, redeploying staff inside the firm) and Cook Group's in-house AI university are ways of *leveraging digital knowledge inside the firm* and raising the workforce's digital maturity rather than replacing it.

## The AI companies' motives (39:17–42:27)

Morgan Jay notes that [[Anthropic]] is in the Raise US coalition and asks whether Dario Amodei, *"very outspoken about the risks of AI,"* acts from altruism. Raimondo: *"you'd have to ask him."* Her view: *"if all they're doing is creating a technology which puts the rest of us out of work, then why would we allow them to do that?… That social contract doesn't work… I think they understand it's bad for their business if they don't figure out some solutions to the potential harms of AI."* And: *"I think it is a problem to just come out and say all the bad stuff about AI without offering any solutions because that does create what's been created, mass panic."* Raise US wants the first case where *"these 200 workers were going to be laid off but now they have this job,"* or a company that keeps hiring entry-level workers because of an incentive: *"we're just trying to give people hope that there are answers… I would think they're funding us so we can help find some solutions."*

[[2026-06-05-nadella-hoffman-possible-ai-future-of-the-firm|Satya Nadella on Possible]] makes the same link between rhetoric and consent from inside the industry: AI companies lost *"social permission"* by saying white-collar jobs are gone, and should name the new jobs, their wages and what people can train for.

## Audience questions (42:27–57:55)

**What to study.** An academic asks what to tell students who ask why they should write an essay an AI can write, and what to major in. Raimondo: struggling through economics problem sets is *"how I learned to like think like an economist… I don't think I would have learned that if I didn't struggle through all those problem sets."* Her advice to her own children: *"learn how to use the tools big time and always be ahead of it… learn how to deal with constant change and unpredictability… if you only thrive in situations of great certainty and security, this is going to be a tough road,"* and *"it's a cool time to be an entrepreneur."* Holcomb: *"knowledge plus experiences where you gain wisdom"*, learning from failure, and a working life that is no longer *"go to school, graduate, get a job, retire."* See [[durable-skills]] and, on learning through effort, [[ai-deskilling]].

**Knowledge work.** A first-year student: *"Ahead of tomorrow's IPO, Anthropic has forecasted market opportunity at $40 trillion, the aggregate of all knowledge work in the world essentially. Do you think that that's a reasonable assumption?"* Holcomb calls the hypothetical *"a lot of gobbledygook… not your question,"* and turns to *"the pragmatic… the to-do list"* and to pathways: his blacksmith great-grandfather, who moved from wooden wagon wheels to rubber tyres, and his father, who went from community college to Purdue and retired from Rolls-Royce, *"in the span of two generations."* [[2026-10-28-damodaran-anthropic-ipo-valuation|Damodaran's valuation of Anthropic]], made from the same prospectus, sizes the total market for AI products and services at $5–10 trillion.

**"Truth in Numbers."** A Kennedy School student asks what Raimondo's 2011 Rhode Island pension reform teaches for the AI transition. As state treasurer she wrote a report, *Truth in Numbers*, that laid out the underfunding *"in math"*, *"the consequences of inaction"*, principles and some options, without telling readers *"the solution"*, and took it on the road to union halls (*"800 angry firefighters"*). The reform passed, she says, by 36–2 in the Senate and 55–3 in the House. Her lesson: *"Change is scary and hard and you must educate people as to why the change is necessary and what the consequences of inaction are and why it matters to everyone… you need to develop trust which comes with transparency."* Then: *"We should do a truth in numbers for AI and take our show on the road."* Her caveat: *"the problem in politics today is people don't even agree on the facts or what the problem is."*

[[2026-11-21-dw-news-which-jobs-are-still-safe-from-ai|DW News's report on which jobs are still safe]] covers the same move from office work to care and the trades from the side of apprentices in Germany, and ends by saying that retraining office workers for jobs AI cannot do seems appropriate while governments wait to see how to respond.
