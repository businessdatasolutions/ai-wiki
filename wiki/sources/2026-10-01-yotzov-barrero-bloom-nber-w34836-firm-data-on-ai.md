# Firm Data on AI (NBER Working Paper 34836, October 2026 revision)

## TL;DR

An NBER working paper by seventeen economists at central banks and universities, led by Ivan Yotzov (Bank of England), Jose Maria Barrero (ITAM) and Nicholas Bloom (Stanford). They put the same AI questions to senior executives through central-bank business surveys in seven countries. This is the **third version** (*"February 2026, Revised October 2026"*), and its headline figures differ from the first two.

1. **78% of firms use some AI technology** (seven-country average, May-July 2026; p. 15). At the start of 2026, in the first wave, the figure was 69% (p. 4). Country figures run from 95% (US) to 56% (South Africa).
2. **AI spending is very concentrated.** US firms expect to spend about $2,000 per employee on AI in 2026. **1.8% of US firms and 1.3% of UK firms account for half of all AI spending** (p. 19). The abstract rounds this to *"just 2% of firms accounting for over 50% of total spending"* (p. 2). This is a US and UK finding only.
3. **Executives report almost no employment effect so far.** 87% report no impact over the past three years, and the average is about zero (p. 21). They expect a small fall, -0.4%, over the next three years, but more than 40% expect some change either way: churn rather than net loss (pp. 21-22).
4. **Executives report a small productivity effect so far, and expect more.** AI raised their firms' labour productivity (sales per employee) by about 0.5% over three years, or *"0.2% a year"* (pp. 2, 5, 23; Table 4, p. 50: 0.46%). They expect 1.7% over the next three years, about 0.6% a year (p. 23). These are self-reports.
5. **The paper explains why adoption figures disagree.** Using UK data, it shows that firm size mix, weighting scheme and the respondent's position each move measured adoption by *"between five and twelve percentage points"*, and that they compound (pp. 16-17).

## Method and definitions

- **Who answered.** Senior executives (CEOs, CFOs, senior finance managers) in existing central-bank and university panels: the Atlanta Fed's Survey of Business Uncertainty (US), the Bank of England's Decision Maker Panel (UK), the Bundesbank Online Panel (Germany), Macquarie University's BOSS (Australia), the Bank of Canada's Business Leaders' Pulse, Banco de México's EMAER and the South African Reserve Bank's survey (pp. 8-14). Respondents are unpaid and identity-checked, which the authors set against paid online executive panels (p. 10).
- **Fieldwork.** Wave 1: November 2025 to January 2026 (US, UK, Germany, Australia). Wave 2: May to July 2026, all seven countries (pp. 4, 9). Germany's employment and productivity figures still come from January 2026 (pp. 20, 22). A Bank of Korea survey of 749 firms (89% adoption) is reported apart, in Appendix C (p. 84).
- **The adoption question.** *"Which of the following artificial intelligence technologies, if any, does your business currently use?"* Firms tick any of: data processing using machine learning, visual content creation, generative AI, image processing, robotics and autonomous vehicles, other (pp. 15, 33). The question asks about current use, not use in production.
- **Category change between versions.** The first two versions asked about *"text generation using large language models"* and listed robotics and autonomous vehicles separately (March version, p. 12). This version asks about *"Generative AI"* and merges the two.
- **Effects** are executives' own estimates on a five-point scale, coded to ±7.5%, ±2.5% and 0 (pp. 20, 23). Productivity means sales per employee.
- **Funding:** ESRC, Smith Richardson Foundation, Stanford Impact Labs (p. 1).

## Scope and reliability

- **Self-report by executives.** Adoption, spending and effects are what executives say about their own firm. The paper finds CEOs and CFOs report adoption four to seven points higher than other respondents, and cannot tell whether they see more or claim more (p. 17).
- **The headline moved between versions for two reasons.** New data (wave 2, three more countries) and a coding change. In the first version, US firms that skipped the adoption question counted as non-users, giving 78%; this version treats them as missing, giving 94% (November 2025) and 95% (June 2026) (footnote 13, p. 15). So the US figure in the [[2026-07-01-stanford-del-adoption-monitor|Adoption Monitor]] (77.5%) and this paper's US figure (95%) describe the same survey under different coding.
- **Weighting is mixed.** Four surveys are employment-weighted, three equal-weighted, and the seven are then averaged by sample size (p. 33). The 78% is not a population-weighted share of firms. On UK data, employment weighting gives 88%, business-count weighting 83% (p. 16).
- **The sample favours larger firms** than official statistics do. The UK panel covers firms with ten or more employees; Mexico's covers establishments with more than 100 workers (pp. 8, 13).
- **The employee survey has gone.** The March version contrasted executives with US employees, who expected AI to *raise* employment by 0.5% (March version, p. 2). That comparison is not in this version.
- **Working paper, not peer-reviewed** (p. 1).

## Dynamic-capabilities reading

- **`digital-sensing/digital-scouting`**: a standing, twice-yearly scan of how many firms use AI, how much they spend and what executives expect, across seven countries.
- **`digital-transforming/improving-digital-maturity`**: the paper measures how far firms have got (use, spend per employee, executive use), and finds the intensity highly concentrated: a small share of firms makes most of the investment.
