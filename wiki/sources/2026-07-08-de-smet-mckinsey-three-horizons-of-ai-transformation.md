# De Smet et al. — From adoption to impact: Three horizons of AI transformation (McKinsey Quarterly, Jul 2026)

## TL;DR

A *McKinsey Quarterly* article of 8 July 2026 by seven **[[McKinsey & Company]]** authors: senior partners Aaron De Smet and **[[Tanguy Catlin]]**, partner Drew Goldstein, associate partner **[[Holly Price]]**, data scientists Laura Pineault and Nicolette Rainone, and Susann Almasi. It reports a survey of 750 employees and leaders run from February to April 2026. Price discussed it on **[[2026-11-24-price-mckinsey-talks-talent-employees-more-ready-for-ai|McKinsey Talks Talent]]** four and a half months later. That episode gave the headline numbers and left out the method; this page holds the method.

1. **Three horizons.** Organizations are grouped by how deeply AI is built into daily work. *Enablement* gives employees general-purpose tools. *Automation* uses AI to automate and improve existing cross-functional workflows. *Reinvention* redesigns roles, workflows and operating models from scratch. Of 582 leaders who could be classified: 46%, 43% and 11%.
2. **A readiness gap.** 70% of all respondents say they are personally ready to use AI; 27% of leaders say their organization is ready for the people and culture shifts an *"agentic future"* needs.
3. **Organizational readiness weighs about twice as much.** It accounts for 48% of the difference between leaders who report capturing value and those who do not; personal readiness for 25%. The page does not say how this split was computed.
4. **Value rises by horizon.** Leaders reporting enterprise value: 13% in enablement, 24% in automation, 48% in reinvention. Leaders saying their organization is *not* ready: 84%, 68% and 44%.
5. **Different factors matter in each horizon** (Exhibits 4–6, below). Enablement: workflow redesign (32% of leaders report value where it is high, against 6% where it is low) and daily work adaptation. Automation: leader AI fluency (35% vs 9%), road map, organizational redesign, resource allocation, and support and training (30% vs 9%). Reinvention: organizational behavior shifts (61% vs 30%).
6. **Trust is the one factor in every horizon.** Anxiety about AI-related change is highest among middle managers (one in four, against one in five individual contributors). Low-trust respondents are 1.5 times as likely to be anxious. The authors separate building trust from reducing anxiety: reassurance that nothing will change can lower anxiety without earning trust.
7. **Four behaviors set reinvention apart from enablement.** The authors drew them from McKinsey's Organizational Health Index:
   - business-led tech investment tied to value;
   - innovation championed from the top and spread across the organization;
   - performance transparency and process documentation;
   - cross-functional collaboration with continuous learning.
8. **The electrification analogy.** Factories that swapped steam engines for electric motors and kept the old layout gained little. The gains came when factories were redesigned around electricity. The article reads AI the same way.

The organizations that capture value, the authors argue, concentrate on the areas with most value and rewire workflows there. They also run AI as organizational change. Spreading pilots, or automating today's processes, is the pattern they warn against.

## The method, and what it allows

The *"About the research"* sidebar and endnotes give the method; [the raw file](../../raw/articles/2026-07-08-mckinsey-from-adoption-to-impact-three-horizons-of-ai-transformation.md) has it in full.

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Instrument | AI Individual & Organizational Readiness Assessment Panel Survey |
| Respondents | 750 English-speaking employees across job levels, five regions; all had to report using AI in their work |
| Fieldwork | February–April 2026 |
| Organizational readiness and value questions | Leaders only: executive team, top management, middle management (608) |
| Classified into one horizon | 717 respondents; within-horizon analysis on 582 leaders (267 enablement, 252 automation, 63 reinvention) |
| "Ready" / "high" | Mean score ≥ 4 on a composite of 5-point agreement items |
| Enterprise value | Composite of four self-reported items: bottom line, cost savings, employee experience, customer outcomes |
| Per-horizon predictors | Statistically significant positive predictors from separate regression models for organizational and personal readiness |

Four limits follow from it. The article states the first two itself.

- **The 46 / 43 / 11 split is not the market.** Recruitment targeted organizations in the more advanced horizons, so every horizon had respondents. The authors say the estimates *"may not reflect overall market prevalence."* The Talks Talent episode presented the split as where organizations are; it is where this sample is.
- **Respondents speak for themselves, not their firms.** The sidebar says the responses are not representative accounts of the organizations. A horizon is one respondent's view: 85% judged their whole organization, 11% a unit, 4% a team.
- **Value is self-reported, by leaders, at one point in time.** The pattern by horizon and the low-versus-high comparisons are cross-sectional associations. The article's *"5.3 times more likely"* is 32% divided by 6% within one horizon. It is not an effect of redesigning workflows.
- **The reinvention group is small.** Its 48% value figure and its two predictors rest on 63 leaders.

The 70% against 27% comparison also mixes populations. The 70% covers all 750 respondents rating themselves. The 27% covers 608 leaders rating their organization, from a sample screened for people who already use AI at work.

## What each horizon asks of leaders

- **Enablement.** Most use is drafting, summarizing meetings and preparing for client work. Employees save time, but *"freed-up capacity"* often goes to personally interesting work that is not an enterprise priority. That is the [[micro-productivity-trap|micro-productivity trap]] in McKinsey's terms. The fix: formal workflow changes, explicit expectations about working differently, freed time steered to business priorities, and baseline fluency rather than advanced skills.
- **Automation.** These firms automate cross-functional processes, for example AI that routes customer requests, drafts answers and escalates hard cases to people. What holds them back is that they optimize the current state instead of starting from a blank sheet. Structural and resource changes create perceived winners and losers, and some leaders resist.
- **Reinvention.** The constraint shifts to leaders' ability to imagine new ways of working. Collective behavior, the way leaders *"run the place"*, matters most.
- **For every leader**, four questions: where AI will create value, how work must change to capture it, which skills the leader needs, and how to manage *"a human-and-agents system."*

## The same work, told twice

The Talks Talent episode (24 November 2026) is Price's own account of this article, so it [[2026-11-24-price-mckinsey-talks-talent-employees-more-ready-for-ai|depends on this page]] rather than supporting it. Three differences:

- The episode presents the 46 / 43 / 11 split as where organizations stand. The article says the sample was tilted toward advanced horizons on purpose.
- The episode stresses employees' worry that leaders do not want to hear what is not working. The article frames the same finding as trust in the organization's support through change.
- The article's 48% / 25% decomposition and the per-horizon tables do not appear in the episode.

[[Tanguy Catlin]] made the redesign-over-deployment argument a day after publication, in [[2026-07-09-catlin-mckinsey-podcast-real-ai-advantage|The real AI advantage]] on The McKinsey Podcast. That interview cites McKinsey's *Rewired*, not this survey. Being the same author, it is not independent support.

## Debates and supersession

- **Distribution versus sample.** The 46 / 43 / 11 split was quoted on Talks Talent as a picture of organizations. By the article's own sidebar it reflects a sample recruited toward advanced horizons. The wiki treats it as a sample split. The [[ai-maturity-measurement-comparison|maturity thread]] now records it that way.
- **"Nearly twice as important."** The 48% / 25% shares come with no method (a dominance analysis or similar is likely, but unstated). They describe how much each readiness score accounts for differences in leaders' self-reported value. They do not show that improving organizational readiness would raise value twice as much.
- **Not yet published.** The authors promise a follow-up with examples of leaders driving the change. A later ingest should link it with a `depends-on` edge.
