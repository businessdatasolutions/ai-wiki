# Mansour (Lenny's Podcast) — The PM as shared brain, and why the AI-builder squads went back to their roles

> Sherif Mansour is Head of AI and Product Craft at Atlassian (the company behind Jira and Confluence), where he has spent 17 years building and scaling some of the most successful enterprise products in history. He joined Atlassian as its first PM on Confluence, eventually becoming its first Distinguished PM, and now leads both the craft of product management across the company and Atlassian's AI product strategy.
>
> We discuss:
> 1. Why a PM's #1 job is to build a "shared brain"
> 2. The artist, the scientist, and the GM: three types of PM, and how to tell which one you are
> 3. Why PM roles are growing fastest in the AI era
> 4. Why Atlassian is hiring more juniors than ever
> 5. The most common mistake new PM managers make
>
> *— Channel description, Lenny's Podcast*

## TL;DR

An 82-minute interview on [[Lenny's Podcast]], published 22 November 2026. Host **Lenny Rachitsky** talks with **Sherif Mansour**, Head of AI and Product Craft at **Atlassian**. Mansour has been there 17 years, joined as Confluence's first PM, became its first Distinguished PM, and now sits on the executive team as head of AI. The title comes from one case study (23:42–28:41): Atlassian put PMs and designers into squads where *"everyone was the AI builder,"* and within about a month they had gone back to their own jobs. Most of the episode is about the PM craft in a large organisation.

What it adds to the wiki:

1. **The PM's job is to fill context gaps, not to make the decisions.** *"They don't own decision-making. PMs own decision-making velocity"* (29:29). The PM builds a **shared brain** with the team across customer, product-and-market and business context, so engineers and designers can decide for themselves.
2. **The AI-builder experiment and its result.** *"We realized throughput's not our problem. Direction is our problem. Throughput's getting cheaper"* (25:03). Specialists still matter: *"AI is really good at amplifying your strengths and equally it amplifies your weaknesses"* (27:48).
3. **Job-market figures from the host**, from his forthcoming state-of-the-market report: more open PM roles than any other role, engineering openings slowing, design flat, about 108 open user-research roles at tech companies worldwide (22:20–23:19).

Bolting AI onto an established product's existing workflows is often right. Atlassian's figure: *"nearly 10 million agents that run in loops every month"* in Jira and Confluence (48:59).

## The PM's job: a shared brain, not the decisions (1:24–11:25)

Mansour limits his answer to *"any medium to large organization… I think it's quite different to a startup."* The largest part of the job is *"filling context. They're filling context with humans and I guess these days with agents too."* He names three kinds of context: customer, product and market, and *"the business that you operate in."* Scaling product teams was the hardest thing he has done at Atlassian: *"everything breaks, trust breaks, shared brains break, us and them culture starts forming. In any scaled organization, more than 20, 30 people, you're going to run into this"* (3:09).

His advice to PMs is to *"stay away from the Jira backlog"*: if you think one card matters more than another, *"there's some context that you have that the rest of your team doesn't."* Practices he names: no customer interviews without engineers in the room (*"your aha moments and your empathy… is, you know, 100 times better than someone who's just handing over reports"*), designers co-creating rather than handing over designs, a PM who had every engineer try different AI tools and report back, and engineers staffing the booths at Atlassian's customer event in Europe. A PM in a squad *"has no reports"* and must *"influence without authority."* There are two operating models, handing over a spec or getting the team bought into the vision. In the second, *"the best engineers are almost like suggesting their own features."* His example is from 2010. Confluence customers marked meeting notes with red and green stars for open and done items. An engineer who sat in on the call proposed a checkbox in the page. At the time, *"putting tasks in a document editor was not a concept that existed."*

## Three PM types: general manager, scientist, artist (11:25–18:09)

The **PM craft triangle** was created by Joff Redfern when he joined Atlassian. Its corners describe how a PM naturally does product discovery:

- **Scientist**: hypothesis-driven, *"AB testing a lot,"* typical of growth teams.
- **General manager**: thinks in *"TAM and business and profit and revenue."*
- **Artist**: *"emotionally vision-led and experience-led"*. Artists may start with a solution and work back to the problems.

Atlassian uses it in hiring and calibration. Asked how to improve their favourite product, GMs size a new business opportunity, scientists form a hypothesis and test variants, and artists *"paint some visual vision and then kind of back their way into what the problems are."* Few people do all three well; many do all three at a basic level, which suits zero-to-one projects and startups. Larger organisations *"have the luxury"* of specialists, so the question for a team is *"do we need more of an artist, a scientist, general manager?"* rather than *"throwing PM bodies at the problem."* Moving a struggling PM to a different type of project often revives them.

Mansour calls himself a *"very heavy strong artist PM"* and *"the worst general manager type,"* complemented by Jamil (Valliani, per the description). His advice is to run towards your strengths, *"celebrate your weaknesses publicly,"* and build a complementary team, above a baseline of competence in each area. Early in a career the unlock is *"an opportunity that is just above their level at the right time"* that plays to the person's strengths (18:38). Atlassian runs dual-track careers, with Distinguished PMs and engineers who *"work at the executive level"* without managing (1:00:49).

## PM openings, and the AI-builder squads (22:19–28:48)

Lenny previews his report on the tech job market: PMs *"seem to be benefiting most in the AI era. We are seeing the most open PM roles of any role."* Engineering openings are slowing, design is *"very flat,"* and user research has *"108"* open roles *"globally at tech companies right now"* (22:48–23:19).

Mansour's explanation is the case study in the title. A year earlier, *"it felt like the narrative was everyone's becoming an AI builder,"* defined as *"someone who spends most of their time building software with AI."* Atlassian *"moved some of our product manager and designers to a team, a few teams"* in which *"everyone was the AI builder, there was no PM or designer,"* though members came from different crafts. In the first weeks a PM reported, *"We'll move faster than ever before."* After about a month he came back: *"everyone's gone back to doing their traditional jobs."* The lesson he draws: *"throughput's not our problem. Direction is our problem. Throughput's getting cheaper."* His internal post was titled *"direct the ship, don't row it."* Quite a few Atlassian PMs have committed code, and in a startup or a zero-to-one project *"everyone needs to do anything."* But where a company has *"hundreds, thousands, tens of thousands of engineers,"* the problem is direction: *"there's more throughput, so I need more people to steer the ship"* (25:58).

He compares it with the Balsamiq mock-up era, when everyone could make mock-ups: *"PMs are some of the worst solution designers in the world… because you have access to the tool doesn't mean you're an expert at the domain."* *"You can only get the AI to do something as good as your knowledge of the specialization is."* His image: everyone's circles in the Venn diagram *"are getting bigger,"* but *"the circles aren't overlapping,"* so the roles do not compress and specialists are still needed for *"quality and taste"* (28:20–28:41). See [[expert-generalist]] for the corpus's sources that report roles merging.

## What PMs should do with AI (28:48–31:04)

Start from the team's context gap, then ask whether AI helps close it. If the gap is vision, prototyping may help. If the team lacks market understanding, *"you might send off a bunch of agents to do some analysis and build that with a team."* Lenny summarises: shipping code to production is *"very unlikely"* to be the bottleneck, except in a startup or a new team.

## Hiring: artists, culture, and juniors (31:04–46:05)

Three things are trending up in Atlassian's PM hiring:

1. **Artist PMs**, *"because taste has become more important than ever before when everyone has access to the same intelligence."* Artists *"are more data informed than data driven"* and pick up the whiteboard marker. Mansour does not expect *"the agent interface"* to be *"the final interface"*: *"in a world where… more people can build more stuff cheaper, faster, easier, experience matters more because that becomes one of your possible differentiators"* (39:57–40:32). In take-home Looms from the next APM cohort, one candidate walked through the TAM and the business case before any experience, and another walked the end-to-end journey and its *"delighters."*
2. **Cultural change.** *"If you define culture is how work gets done,"* an established team reinventing its work with AI needs people who bring new ways of working, so interviewers ask candidates how they work with AI, past *"I use it to summarize stuff."*
3. **Juniors.** The misconception he debunks most often with established organisations is *"you don't need to hire juniors anymore."* Their problem is a workforce that must unlearn old ways of working, and *"it's much easier to grab someone out of college"* who has *"not known any better."* His 12-year-old son went from a visual coding language *"straight to vibe coding"* and dismisses models (*"Dad, this Gemini wasn't that good"*). *"Juniors are not great at identifying AI slop because they don't have the domain expertise,"* while seniors *"can pick AI slop a mile away,"* so the aim is *"a complementary workforce between the juniors and the seniors."* In Atlassian's **AI builder weeks**, *"all the crafts stop and they tinker with AI for a week"*. *"The juniors… tend to be showing all this coolness and… demos,"* and *"the seniors tend to focus on identifying slop, evals, quality problems."* On adoption he cites Atlassian research, internal and with customers: *"something like almost 30, I think it's 38% exact… of juniors are more likely to adopt new tools than the seniors."* Seniors *"are getting more quality and better productivity out of the ones they're using, but they're less likely to try different ways of working"* (37:43–38:00).

On design hiring being flat, Mansour's *"leading hypothesis"* is that *"the market still hasn't got past the aha moment"* that experience matters most. At Atlassian, *"design has never played a more important role in road map driving"* in his 17 years (45:12).

## Starting with the solution, and bolting AI on (46:05–56:01)

Two cases in which starting with a solution is right:

- **Discovery through a canvas.** About three years ago Atlassian debated whether to ship a general chat and let customers build their own agents, or a feature such as *"a summarize button on top of Confluence pages."* The transcript renders the product as "Robo chat" and "Revo agents" (see Source quality). The general canvas showed what to productise. Customers used chat to group sticky notes on Confluence whiteboards into themes, a poor experience in chat, so a select-and-group feature was built. Customers run *"nearly 10 million agents… in loops every month inside Jira and Confluence"* (48:59). Some agent uses stay open-ended, and others become native features such as AI triage. He calls this *"paving the cow path"*: a customer's manual workaround becomes a product capability.
- **Product strategy from assets.** *"Write down what assets you have today, then write down your beliefs."* His example is Amazon's bookstore leading to the everything store.

On bolting AI on, he accepts *"reimagine the workflow"* as an exercise but says that for an established product *"the best way to reinvent your product to be AI native is to understand the flows that exist in your product and literally bolt on AI to them."* Customers have *"billions of business workflows"* modelled in Jira, many outside software, from formal Workflows to informal *"lowercase w workflows."* Atlassian *"added the ability to add agents to columns and cards"*: *"if I was building an AI native work tracking tool, that's probably what I would do anyway."* The job is to move customers *"from AI novice to AI native"* (55:20). In the lightning round he describes Gemini in Google Maps (on CarPlay, in Australia) as *"a lesson in product workflow proximity"*. Lenny asks, *"Did they just bolt AI onto it?"*

## Nuance, managers and founders (56:01–1:10:51)

Mansour's stated aim for the episode is nuance. *"Every advice you've probably got on how to do product, someone probably has a counter, totally opposite advice that works just as good,"* so the context in which advice worked matters. Lenny mentions his MCP server with all his podcast transcripts and newsletter posts (lennysdata.com), and a tool by Noam Segal that *"shows both sides of every debate."*

- **Managers.** Lenny's counter-narrative is that managers matter. Atlassian has both models, managers of 10–15 and managers of three or four who also own a project. The minimum span is *"three or four,"* most are *"in the eight or nine."* The most common mistake of new PM managers: *"they go too hands off too quickly."* The best managers *"go in with the team and help them get through outcomes"* and lead by example, *"more important in the world of AI"* (1:02:58–1:04:02).
- **Visionary founders.** Working with one is *"like me and you watching a movie and they're on… chapter 10… and you're on chapter three."* In his experience they have been right *"99% of the time"* because they have thought about it longest. His approach is to *"assume they're right and assume you're wrong and try to fill that context gap."*
- **Disagreeing.** State the case, then ask *"who owns this decision?"* Founders often answer *"I disagree but I want you to decide."* Present both sides to the team, and ask in reviews *"Why would we not do this?"* Lenny cites "flash tags", a framework he credits to someone the ASR renders as "Dash Shaw", for marking an opinion as an FYI, a suggestion, a recommendation or a plea. Mansour puts the clarifying question about a stakeholder's conviction on the PM.

## Open by default, and a remote-work hypothesis (1:11:37–1:21:38)

Asked about an Atlassian ritual, Mansour names openness: *"every exec review is usually recorded, available to search and find in Loom and ask questions and, you know, run the AI agent through it."* *"Sharing context is key in the world of AI… there by definition it means there's more context that the AI has access to and the humans have access to"* (1:18:15–1:18:32). Atlassian lets staff work anywhere. Its data scientists are testing his hypothesis that *"those that are remote are more likely to get more outcomes out of AI than those that are colocated,"* because remote staff write and share more digitally. Lenny's report finds that remote work *"has plateaued"* at *"10, 15, 20% across industries."*

## Dynamic-capabilities reading

- **`digital-transforming/redesigning-internal-structures`.** The AI-builder squads were a team-based structure without PM or designer roles, and they reverted within a month. The PM craft triangle is used to compose teams by type of PM. Atlassian also describes dual-track careers that put individual contributors at the executive level, and manager spans of three or four up to eight or nine.
- **`digital-transforming/improving-digital-maturity`.** External recruiting of digital natives as policy: more juniors than seniors, hired so that an established workforce learns new ways of working with AI. AI builder weeks and interview questions about how candidates work with AI serve the same aim.
- **`digital-seizing/rapid-prototyping`.** Starting with a solution: ship a general canvas (chat, customer-built agents), watch what customers build, and productise the common cases (*"paving the cow path"*). Prototypes are used to clarify a vision.
- **`strategic-renewal/organizational-culture`.** *"Culture is how work gets done"*, and hiring for cultural additions is treated as the lever for changing it.
- **`contextual/internal-enablers`.** An open-by-default culture, with exec reviews recorded and searchable by people and AI agents. Cross-functional context-sharing (engineers in customer interviews and at booths) is the PM's main job.
