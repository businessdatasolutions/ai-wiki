# Price et al. — Why Employees Are More Ready for AI Than Their Companies (McKinsey Talks Talent, Nov 2026)

> Business leaders have been preoccupied with AI adoption, but employees are more open to AI than their organizations might think. And while job loss naturally looms large, it isn't necessarily what worries employees most.
>
> "They're more concerned that it's not working as intended and that leaders don't care what they have to say about it," says McKinsey Associate Partner Holly Price, coauthor of the recent McKinsey Quarterly article "From adoption to impact."
>
> On this episode of _McKinsey Talks Talent,_ Price joins Senior Partner Brooke Weddle, Partner Bryan Hancock, and Global Editorial Director Lucia Rahilly to discuss the disconnect between employee and organizational readiness for AI—and how leaders can bridge it to begin generating meaningful value at scale.
>
> — channel description, McKinsey & Company (chapter list and links omitted)

## TL;DR

A ~29-minute episode of *McKinsey Talks Talent* on the **[[McKinsey & Company]]** channel, published 24 November 2026. The guest is **[[Holly Price]]**, a McKinsey associate partner and coauthor of the *McKinsey Quarterly* article *"From adoption to impact: Three horizons of AI transformation"* (8 July 2026). The article is ingested as [[2026-07-08-de-smet-mckinsey-three-horizons-of-ai-transformation|De Smet et al. 2026]] (added 2026-11-27), and this episode is her account of it. The hosts are **[[Lucia Rahilly]]**, **[[Brooke Weddle]]** and **[[Bryan Hancock]]**. The captions are auto-generated, with names corrected at acquire time.

The episode reports a survey that used McKinsey's new **"AI readiness assessment"**, which measures individual and organisational readiness separately. The figures are spoken; none is shown on screen. Neither sample size, countries, field dates nor definitions are given (*"an international survey"*). The article gives them: 750 English-speaking employees who use AI at work, surveyed February to April 2026. Recruitment was tilted toward organisations in the advanced horizons, so the 46 / 43 / 11 split describes the sample, not the market.

**1. Three horizons (1:28–3:20).** *"Organizations are falling into three different levels of AI maturity"*:

| Horizon | Share | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Enablement | 46% | *"Offering employees access to general purpose AI tools. This is more about individual augmentation."* |
| 2. Automation | 43% | *"Starting to use AI to automate and improve existing workflows."* |
| 3. Reinvention | 11% | *"Truly reimagining how work is done and how value is going to be created… in most cases it is a true clean sheet."* Value can be *"cost savings, innovation, growth, employee engagement."* |

**2. What the few with real value do differently (3:40–4:55).** Price names three things:
- they pinpoint where AI will create value, not *"just adding tech and new tools into their stack"*;
- they redesign processes, *"in some cases radically,"* end to end;
- they treat change management *"as mission-critical, not an afterthought"*: redesigned roles, multifaceted communication, transparency, incentives to follow the new process.

Hancock quotes a client: *"this isn't just like the roll out of the latest version of Excel."*

**3. Individual readiness and anxiety (5:56–7:26).** *"Roughly 70% of employees surveyed said that they were open and ready to navigate the AI transition."* Against that, *"one in four middle managers are feeling anxious about AI and one in five employees overall."* Individual readiness needs:
- tools and licences (*"do we even have licenses for this technology?"*);
- knowing how to use AI in one's own role;
- an intention to use it;
- seeing leaders role-model it;
- **trust in the organisation**.

In confidential focus groups, *"people do have a lot to say about concerns and low trust in the organization."*

**4. Enablement as trust-building (7:51–9:20).** Weddle: many clients keep enablement going *"because it helps to build trust. It also helps to upskill"*, while pushing a top-down reinvention agenda. A client example follows; the edit cuts from Weddle to Price as it starts, so the speaker is uncertain. When the clean sheet was outlined, leadership and the CEO recognised *"they're not ready to go that far."* The client chose automation of manual administrative work instead, and announced: *"we are not cutting any jobs related to this."*

**5. Four trust moves (9:30–11:20).**
- **Lead with transparency.** *"At the majority of my clients, there is a massive disconnect between senior leaders and the front line."*
- **Have a plan.** Senior leaders say *"We don't have a strategy. We don't have a road map… How am I supposed to tell employees and reassure them?"*
- **"Don't talk about it, be about it."** There is *"a growing skepticism"* about communications not followed by reskilling, job protection and redeployment.
- **Walk the halls.** The quote the description leads with: *"Sure, I don't want to lose my job. I'm actually not that worried about that. What I'm more concerned about is it's not working as intended and leaders do not care what I have to say about it."*

**6. Leaders' own fluency (11:21–16:01).** Asked how they personally use AI, CEOs of large companies point to *"this guy on the seventh floor who works in technology"*, or say *"I've been using it personally to plan a vacation."* *"In many cases, I'm seeing people use AI for the sake of using AI and it's not that great what the output is."* Weddle: with digital, a C-suite leader could leave it to the CTO or CIO and *"stay in your lane"*; with AI *"that is totally not acceptable now."*

The remedy is **reverse mentoring**: junior employees coach senior leaders. Hancock went through it at McKinsey, where engagement managers coached tenured partners. Even as a self-described user he came away with *"four or five things"*. It was *"a super powerful and super simple way of… increasing the fluency without a big learning program."*

**7. Translators, and ideas that need no redesign (16:04–17:47).** Price expects demand for people who translate frontier tools into day-to-day work. She mentions two:
- something *[[Anthropic]]* *"just launched"* for nonprofits, rendered in the captions as *"Claude Core"*, with junior people on one-year secondments to train organisations up (the name is unconfirmed);
- an internal *"pit crew… able to apply user-centered design."*

Then a favourite simple idea, probably Hancock's (the camera is on him): a tech company's HR function uses AI to monitor leaders' speeches *"to see where they're on point and where they're deviating"* and coach them. It *"didn't require a 4-month re-engineering of a workflow."*

**8. Scaling without bureaucracy (17:51–20:31).** Weddle: not *"some heavy COE or PMO that sits at the center… governing every aspect of AI"*, but a *"nerve center"*: a small group of *"sophisticated multi-disciplinary colleagues"* who spread learnings and reimagine consistently. The same mechanism, she adds, helps get *"rid of bad ideas"* and shows where AI pays. Price: on McKinsey's Organizational Health Index, reinvention-horizon organisations combine employee innovation, knowledge sharing and top-down innovation with *"radical clarity on where you're trying to drive value and how you would measure success."*

**9. A test for reinvention (20:47–21:39).** A year ago CEOs asked about AI *"from a cost cutting lens."* Now *"there's an evolution and an opening of the aperture."* *"A clue that you are on your way to reinvention is… I would probably need two different executive sponsors to be part of a program because I can't just do it by myself in my silo."*

**10. Impact, not activity (21:45–23:03).** *"You're now seeing organizations that had leaderboards to talk about how employees were adopting AI quietly pull back from some of those metrics that just focus on activity and don't focus on outcome."* The measure depends on the process being reinvented: revenue, engagement scores, growth, or survey questions on innovation.

**11. The readiness gap (23:10–25:23).** *"70% of respondents across job levels report that they are personally ready for AI, but only 27% of leaders say that their organization is ready to make the shifts needed for an agentic future."* Price reads the 70% as curiosity (*"I'm open. Let's see where this goes"*), not as an enterprise view she would expect employees to have. Anxiety is *"potentially rising"*: *"not just about job loss, but also about the climate impact of this and confidentiality and bias and security."*

**12. Responsible AI as trust (25:32–27:50).**
- A diverse table: *"It can't just be a legal and IT question… You need senior leaders, you need employee voices, you need HR."*
- Guardrails tied to *"mission, vision, and values"*, not *"performative commitments that you put on a page and never acknowledge again."*
- A standing stance: *"we reserve the right to get smarter."*

Between the front line and senior leaders runs *"the world's most distorted game of telephone"*. Many employees are *"AI curious."*

**13. Questions for leaders (28:08–29:04).** What is the highest-potential part of the organisation to target with reinvention? How will you target it? What change management will it take: *"the comms, the capability building, the role modeling, the incentives"*?

## Debates and supersession

- **The headline compares two different questions.** The 70% is respondents of all job levels rating their *own* readiness. The 27% is *leaders* judging their *organisation's* readiness for *"an agentic future"*. *"Employees are more ready than their companies"* is drawn from those two answers. The episode gives no figure for leaders' personal readiness, or for employees' view of the organisation.
- **The survey is undescribed in the episode.** Sample size, countries, field dates, and how organisations were placed in a horizon (self-report or scoring) are not given. *Resolved 2026-11-27 by [[2026-07-08-de-smet-mckinsey-three-horizons-of-ai-transformation|the article]]:*
  - 750 respondents, all of whom use AI at work;
  - horizons assigned from four self-reported questions;
  - 582 leaders in the horizon split;
  - recruitment deliberately tilted toward advanced horizons, *"may not reflect overall market prevalence."*

  The episode's *"46 percent are in enablement"* should be read as 46% of that sample.
- **"Claude Core" is unconfirmed.** The captions render the programme's name as *"Claude Core"*, and nothing on screen confirms it.
- **Job loss as a secondary worry** is one consultant's reading of confidential focus groups. The same episode reports one in five employees and one in four middle managers as anxious. It cites no figures on the causes of that anxiety beyond the list in claim 11.
- No supersession.
