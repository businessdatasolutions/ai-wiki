# Wang & Xing (Morgan Stanley, Thoughts on the Market) — China's bet on physical AI

> Live from Morgan Stanley's Asia Pacific Summit in Singapore, our China Equity Strategist Laura Wang joins Chief China Economist Robin Xing to explore how physical AI could rewire China's factories and what it takes for that investment to translate into stronger profits and higher incomes.
>
> *— Channel description, Morgan Stanley*

## TL;DR

A 12-minute episode of Morgan Stanley's podcast *Thoughts on the Market*, posted to YouTube as audio over a still title card. It is **part one of a two-part special** recorded at the firm's 25th annual Asia-Pacific Summit in Singapore; part two is not in this video. **Laura Wang**, chief China equity strategist at Morgan Stanley Research, asks the questions and gives the equity figures; **Robin Xing**, chief China economist, gives the macro answers. The card dates the episode **20 November 2026** (a Friday; the closing line says *"Join us Monday for part two"*). YouTube published it on Monday **23 November 2026**, and the filename follows YouTube's `publish_date`.

The question is Wang's: *"more investment is not necessarily more value… what turns smarter machines into better economics, stronger profits, and higher incomes."*

What it adds to the wiki:

1. **A bank's house view, with figures, on physical AI in China.** About **$12 trillion** of incremental *"industry 5.0"* investment over ten years, an addressable market growing from about **$1.4 trillion to $3.2 trillion by 2030**, and a productivity payoff *"more visible from 2028,"* put at about **0.3 percentage point**.
2. **A two-stage sequence.** For now the money goes to bottlenecks, *"chips… upstream equipment, semiconductor, GPUs,"* which are *"low single digit"* of China's total capex. From 2028 Xing expects hardware workarounds and broad deployment in factories.
3. **Where the investment goes.** More than half goes to *"rewiring existing factories,"* turning *"passive automation into proactive adaptive production."*
4. **Policy as the route to margins.** Anti-involution rules and shrinking local-government revenue end local subsidies for new capacity. Xing expects industrial firms' profit margins to rise from 5% over three to five years (the target figure is garbled in the track; see *Source quality*).
5. **Three conditions before households benefit**: investment discipline, a stabilised housing market, and social-welfare reform. Xing calls 2027 *"still tough."*
6. **Equity figures**: earnings growth from 6% a year to above 10% by 2030, and return on equity from about 10% to 12–13%.
7. **"From made in China to made by China"**: foreign factories built in the re-industrialisation cycle *"are running based on Chinese components and machinery."*

The bank's strategists present the bank's own research at the bank's client summit, on the bank's channel. Every figure is stated, none is derived, and the track is auto-generated (see *Source quality*).

## Physical AI as AI's second chapter (0:28–4:11)

Wang opens with the frame from Morgan Stanley's *Emerging Frontiers* research: AI's next chapter *"is not just about what it says to us. It's about what it can do in the physical world. From vehicles on our roads to robots on factory floors."* The research points to *"roughly a 12 trillion US dollars of incremental industry 5.0 investment over the next 10 years across the broader frontier industries,"* and forecasts the addressable market to expand *"from the current 1.4 4 trillion US to 3.2 trillion by 2030."* Her claim for China: *"manufacturing depth and deployment scale could give it a distinctive advantage."*

"Industry 5.0" here means investment in physical AI. Xing later says *"industry 5.0 or physical AI."* The [[industry-4-0]] page records a different use of the term: literature that applies "Industry 5.0" to human-centric and sustainable manufacturing. [[2026-03-25-russell-bradley-mgi-race-takes-off-next-big-arenas|MGI's 2026 arenas update]] defines physical AI as systems that *"sense, think, and act in the real world"* and counts it as part of its hard-tech arenas. For China's installed base, the [[2026-04-28-ai-index-report-2025|AI Index 2025]] puts China at 51.1% of global industrial-robot installations in 2023, and the [[2026-11-10-ai100-ai-goes-mainstream-2026|AI100 2026 report]] says China makes about 80% of humanoid robots.

Xing's framing: phase one of the AI revolution is *"a chat box [chatbot] helping you writing emails"*; physical AI is *"the second chapter… industrywide adoption of AI… shifting from just staying in the clouds going into the real world, factories, ports, logistics."* The reason is structural: *"China is largely a manufacturing based economy. So simply working from the cloud is not so helpful for their productivity."* China is *"betting on"* robotics, factory upgrades, drones and cars over the next five years. [[2026-09-18-reuters-on-assignment-inside-chinas-ai-race|Reuters' China correspondents]] describe the same strategy from the policy side: Beijing pushes AI *"throughout various different sectors of the economy"* to lift productivity against a demographic decline, while the US aims at AGI.

He calls the macro effect a *"two-stage macro equation."* Stage one: *"at this moment, China still face a bottleneck. That's chips, that's upstream equipment, semiconductor, GPUs."* Investment in these *"picks and shovels"* is *"booming, but that's only a small portion of the Chinese economy, about low single digit of their total capex,"* so its contribution to potential growth *"is still limited."* Stage two, from 2028: *"a series of moments in China's AI computing power, in China's hardware… GPU, memory or the super port interconnector"* (the track's rendering), combined *"to use some engineering innovation to bypass single chip limits."* That would open the way for what the track renders as *"AI brands"* (probably AI brains) in robotics and factory upgrades, contributing *"about .3 percentage point"* of productivity gain, with the capex contribution tripling. In the near term the effect is *"still muted in terms of helping China to break out of the deflation trap."* [[2026-11-14-mccarthy-cnn-meanwhile-in-asia-china-data-centers|CNN's Simone McCarthy]] also names high-end chips, limited by US export controls and by China's own import restrictions, as China's largest hurdle. [[2026-09-08-hatzius-gs-macro-impact-of-ai-gdp-productivity-jobs|Goldman Sachs' Jan Hatzius]] makes a parallel estimate for the US: AI investment adds about a tenth of a percentage point to GDP growth now, and a productivity uplift spreads over about a decade.

## From investment to profits: what the money buys and the policy shift (4:11–6:37)

Wang refers to the 2027 outlook the two had just published, which expects China's macroeconomy to stay *"challenged."* Xing states the problem: *"In the past cycles, China was able to make a lot of technology progress winning global market share. But that does not translate to profits for corporates or income for household."* He announces *"three structural factors"* that make this cycle different from previous ones, and names two.

**The nature of the investment.** *"Out of this $12 trillion incremental investment in industry 5.0 or physical AI in China, more than half of that will be rewiring existing factories… making them more intelligent, making this more passive automation into proactive adaptive production and that means robotic industrial software."* [[2026-05-11-ognibeni-ai-agents-cool-demos-vs-real-revenue-china|Ognibeni's e-commerce keynote]] gives a company-level instance of AI in a Chinese factory: Xiaomi's car plant, which he describes as *"so dark because there are no people in there,"* with *"40 cars per hour autonomous production."*

**Policy.** *"80% of China's industrial policy, this subsidy for capacity was done by local government in the past."* Since late 2025 (*"late last year"*), two initiatives: *"so-called anti-involution, changing the KPI, the promotion matrix for local authorities. So now the local mayors don't have the mandate to subsidize all these factories"*; and local governments are *"running out of money"* because a revenue source, rendered "NAN" in the track, *"shrank a lot,"* so they have cut export rebates and clawed back *"high-tech tax privileges."* Near term, *"that's probably negative for certain firms,"* but over the medium term it pushes firms *"to consolidate factories to not just keep expanding but try to keep efficiency and profit margins."* His forecast: over three to five years, industrial firms' profit margin *"could improve from today's 5%"* to a figure the track renders as *"80%"*. [[2026-10-04-chu-liu-dw-the-dip-how-china-is-viewing-the-ai-race|Zongyuan Zoe Liu on DW's The Dip]] discusses the same pair of concerns: she says China invests less than the US partly to avoid stranded assets, and that AI adds to workers' anxiety about *involution*, intensifying competition without gain.

## From profits to households (6:37–9:09)

Wang reports a conclusion of the *Emerging Frontiers* blue paper: *"corporate earnings actually can improve ahead of the household effect."* Xing explains why the household effect lags: *"robotics, they don't drink latte, they don't wear sneakers. So it's not directly creating consumption demand,"* and in the past *"the spill over to household income was limited partially because of excess supply."* Three things must happen:

1. **Investment discipline**: spend on *"rewiring existing factory, making them more intelligent, cost reduction, more efficient, rather than doing all these green field capacity build out to create more redundant factories."*
2. **Housing**: weak consumption is partly *"deflation triggered by housing adjustment."* Tighter rules limit new supply, and Morgan Stanley's property team expects house prices in large cities to stabilise *"by second half next year,"* the second half of 2027.
3. **Social welfare**: Chinese consumers *"save a lot partially because the lack of a proper social welfare system for migrant workers and farmers."* A safety-net reform would *"unlock this precautionary saving."*

*"2027 is still tough."* China is *"very slowly healing"* from balance-sheet repair: local-government fiscal consolidation, household deleveraging, and anti-involution, which hurts capex in the near term. *"By 2028 and onwards"* he expects that healing to be done and the spillover to household income to grow stronger.

## Equity-market figures (9:09–10:02)

Wang adds the equity channel, hoping that part of the household effect *"can come from the equity market investment."* Morgan Stanley expects earnings growth for the Chinese equity market to *"jump from the current 6% on annual basis to above 10% by 2030,"* and return on equity to rise *"from the current 10%-ish level back to above 12%, close to 13%,"* creating a wealth effect alongside wage growth.

## China's global role: "made by China" (10:02–11:27)

Asked whether China's manufacturing gains will provoke trade barriers that derail it, Xing says no: *"China is shifting from made in China to made by China,"* upgrading *"with intelligent factories, with more industrial software, the ecosystem."* In the global *"super cycle"* of re-industrialisation (the track has *"rejoying,"* possibly *reshoring*), *"other countries are also building more and more factories but the foreign factories are running based on Chinese components and machinery. That's actually helping China to gain further market share."* He ends on the imbalance: *"too much supply, too [little] consumer demand."* Only domestic social-welfare reform would *"rebalance that growth model,"* create demand for global firms, and *"manage the trade tensions in a better way."*

For firms that sell into China, source from it or compete with its manufacturers, the episode is a `contextual/external-triggers` source in the [[warner-wager-process-model|Warner & Wäger]] sense. It describes a disruptive technology entering factories, the policy shifts that set its economics (anti-involution, withdrawn local subsidies and export rebates, clawed-back tax privileges), and a timetable for when the effects reach margins, households and trade. It says nothing about how any single firm builds the capability. China's chip constraint and its "made by China" position also bear on the national-autonomy questions recorded on [[ai-sovereignty]].
