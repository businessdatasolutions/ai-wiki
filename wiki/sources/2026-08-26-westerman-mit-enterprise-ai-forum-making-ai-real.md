# Westerman — Making AI Real: The Six Questions Leaders Should Ask (MIT Enterprise AI Forum, Apr 2026)

> In this talk, George Westerman framed the evolving AI conversation as a shift from awareness and innovation to delivering real value and scale across the enterprise. As companies moved beyond pilot projects, he emphasized that a strong model alone was not enough to drive transformation.
>
> Drawing on research he led, Westerman outlined six key questions that leaders must ask—and answer—to genuinely AI‑enable their organizations, with each question tied to a concrete action or capability. Attendees learned how to apply these questions in their own businesses to bridge the gap between experimentation and enterprise‑scale AI impact.
>
> — channel description, MIT Corporate Relations (speaker titles and links omitted)

## TL;DR

A ~42-minute slide talk by **[[George Westerman]]** of the MIT Sloan School of Management at the **2026 MIT Enterprise AI Forum**, held by the MIT Industrial Liaison Program on 15 April 2026. The MIT Corporate Relations channel published it on 26 August 2026; the title slide carries the April date. Westerman's slides give him as Principal Research Scientist; the lower-third gives *Senior Lecturer; Founder, Global Opportunity Forum, MIT Office of Open Learning*. He calls himself *"a reformed engineer"* who now teaches management. The talk ends with Q&A from 38:30. Captions are auto-generated, with names corrected at acquire time. **13 stills** were published (see *Visual canon*).

The thesis is his 2019 *"First Law of Digital Innovation"*: *"Technology changes quickly. Organizations change much more slowly."* The next slide puts it as *"Technology is NOT the problem. Transformation is."* The talk has five parts: two long cases, four short tool examples, a framing (move fast *and* thrive in the long term), six levers with one question each, and an audience self-assessment.

**1. Exponential technology, linear organisations (4:34–6:18).** If technology doubles every 18 months, it is ten times as good every five years and a hundred times every ten. *"If you are still trying to plan and execute your five-year plan, that five-year plan is 10 times out of date on the minute that you start."* Five-year plans still suit an oil platform or a factory. They don't suit customer experience: *"you can't plan."*

**2. Rio Tinto: a fifteen-year journey (6:38–9:00).** A remote operations centre (*"actually a centralized operation center"*), then AI-enhanced exploration, autonomous trucks, autonomous drilling, long-haul trains, and now the supply chain. *"The hard part though was getting our data clean, restructuring how we run our processes, managing to work with the people."* The Mine of the Future was also framed as *"a mine where no miner will ever get hurt again"*. That made the change in the miners' interest: *"you'll still have a job. It just won't be in a drilling machine where you might get killed."*

**3. Dentsu Creative: started before it knew where it was going (9:10–12:12).** Dentsu Creative is among the top five advertising companies, in an industry *"getting totally slammed"* by generative AI. With *"the future state… very, very foggy"*, it worked on three tracks:
- **Bottom-up:** clean tools, a policy and office hours.
- **Middle-out:** IT spotted several employees solving the same problem and built *"an enterprise capability that everybody could use"*.
- **Top-down:** after about a year, the vision of *"intelligent storytelling"*, now worked out with customers, including new business models and measurements.

He is writing it up as a case study. Still 1 has the slide.

**4. Four smaller examples (12:21–18:13).**
- **Synthesia:** the value is not the deepfake but *"integrated content management for video"*. A changed feature, typed in once, updates every video in every language.
- **Harvey:** lawyers have twice cited case law that does not exist. The goal is to *"let your smart people be smart people, not paper pushers, not text typers."*
- **Cresta:** call-centre software that *"whispers"* advice on the agent's screen. *"Every single worker has a coach on every single call."* He cites a randomised trial at *"a company that looks like Cresta, I can't say which one"*: new workers quickly approach the best ones, and *"employee satisfaction went up and employee turnover went down… you're not replacing these people, you're augmenting them."* He does not name the study. Its details match [[2026-04-28-brynjolfsson-li-raymond-generative-ai-at-work|Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's study of a customer-support rollout]] (the least experienced agents gained most, attrition fell). That study used a staggered rollout, not a randomised trial; the identification is the wiki's inference.
- **Adobe:** beyond editing images, it will *"plan and execute an entire marketing campaign autonomously."*

**5. Move fast, and be on the right mountain (18:20–20:22).** *"You got to move fast but at the same time you got to be ready for the long term and those don't always line up."* On the landscape slide (still 2), a firm that moves fast climbs a hill: *"Hey, we got to the top of the hill. Whoops, we're on the wrong hill."* The answer is to swap *planning and execution* for **directed emergence**: *"Set a goal and learn your way towards that goal"* (still 3). Governance becomes *"allocate some resources, give them a week, two weeks, and then learn from what they've done and steer them in a different way."*

**6. The AI growth slope (20:22–22:24).** It comes from research he did *"just last year"* (still 4), with three levels:
1. Individual productivity: tools for everyone, to *"generate energy and ideas"* and learn safety.
2. Specialized roles and tasks: well-defined work such as content, contact centres and coding, usually with a human in the loop.
3. Products and processes: whole processes transformed.

Generative AI alone rarely reaches level 3 yet. In insurance underwriting, firms are *"starting to see let's underwrite x percent of them without a human in the loop, the easiest ones"*. Harder cases get *"a very clean dossier that a human can look at."*

**7. Six levers, six questions, one score each (22:33–34:19).** Still 5 has the framework. At the centre is **shared ambition**. Above it, **governance** and **value capture** are *"how you move up that curve, the directed emergence."* Beneath it sit the enablers **technology platform**, **culture** and **talent**. The audience scores each lever from −5 (*will slow our progress*) to +5 (*will speed our progress*). Stills 6 to 10 have the questions.
- **Shared ambition.** *"How do you want to do business differently?"* Newport News Shipbuilding's first ambition was to *"work faster at higher quality but not pay anybody any more money"*. In *"a unionized shop"*, he says, that did not work. It became *"make it easier to do good work,"* tied to the yard's *"We build good ships"* (still 12).
- **Governance.** *"Is your governance more the steering? Or is it more the brakes?"* The scale problem: *"on the order of every one out of 10, one out of 15 successful pilots actually make it to scale."* HCA Healthcare's steering committee reviews every idea at the start, at the pilot, at three or four hospitals and at scale, and again about every six months: *"Are the models still robust? Are we seeing biases? Are things drifting?"* *"They kill things really fast but they can accelerate things a lot too."* His HCA uses:
  - sepsis detected an hour earlier (*"every hour you delay detecting sepsis is a 7% higher likelihood of death"*);
  - maternal-fetal health during delivery;
  - inventory;
  - the nurse handoff, cut from *"close to 50 minutes to about 12 minutes with 95% better on all the measures that they've chosen."*
- **Value capture.** Five questions asked before any pilot: are you testing in real-world conditions, why will adopters want to adopt (or not), who will support scale-up, what might get in the way, what are the measurements saying. A slide frames them as *"Value Capture starts at the beginning and continues at each stage — even after deployment."* On executive support: *"if you don't have it before you start, don't try."*
- **Technology.** Data, tools, partners, legacy integration. *"They don't boil the ocean."*
- **Culture.** The AI-ready values (still 11): speed, impact, autonomy, openness, plus ethics and empathy for AI. Openness includes non-human collaborators.
- **Talent and careers.** *"They're not saying we're going to fire a lot of people. They're saying we're going to change your job, but we're going to help you make that transition… if you're not telling them, people are thinking the very worst."*

**8. The audience scores (34:24–37:08).** One hand went up for 15 to 30: the founder of an AI-native start-up (*"you didn't have to deal with all the mess"*). More were between 0 and 15. About half did not raise a hand, and he put them below zero. His advice:
- **Below zero:** pick one or two levers, often the employee-side ones (shared ambition, culture), and *"stick on mountain number one"*.
- **Above zero:** push forward, *"because you can accelerate ahead of other people."*

**9. Anywhere in the organisation (37:08–38:14).** *"You do not need to be the top of the company to do it… you can do the faster learning even if everybody else is doing the slower learning."* The conclusion slide (still 13) pairs *move quickly: action and learning* with *think long term: capability and culture*.

**10. Q&A (38:30–41:18).** To a founder rebuilding his systems, he says to help customers solve their own problems: Synthesia sells *"an answer to this constant perennial content management issue."* On value capture: *"make sure that the people who are going to use it are part of the process of developing it… then you don't need to convince somebody. They'll do the convincing."*

**Sources on the slides.** The growth slope is from Melissa Webster and Westerman, *"Generate Value from GenAI with Small 't' Transformations"* ([[MIT Sloan Management Review]], January 2025). The values come from Westerman, Soule and Eswaran, *"Building Digital-Ready Culture in Traditional Organizations"* (MIT SMR, 2019). The First Law is Westerman, MIT SMR, April 2019. The HCA case is *"forthcoming in MIS Quarterly Executive"*. A case-company slide lists DBS, The Home Depot, Rio Tinto, Pfizer, Sysco, HCA Healthcare, Warner Bros. Discovery, Kraft Heinz and Ferrovial. The closing reading list adds *Leading Digital* and HBR articles with Maryam Alavi (2023) and with Sam Ransbotham and Chiara Farronato.

## Dynamic-capabilities reading

- **`digital-seizing/strategic-agility`.** The talk's frame is moving fast and staying on the right mountain at once. Directed emergence (*"set a goal and learn your way towards that goal"*) replaces the five-year plan.
- **`digital-seizing/balancing-digital-portfolios`.** HCA's steering committee asks the same three questions at the idea, the pilot, the multi-hospital stage and at scale, and kills or accelerates projects fast. That is portfolio governance applied idea by idea.
- **`digital-transforming/improving-digital-maturity`.** The AI growth slope is a maturity path in three levels, and the six-lever self-score is a crude maturity check (*"really scientific here"*).
- **`digital-transforming/redesigning-internal-structures`.** Dentsu's middle-out track turns repeated employee innovations into enterprise capabilities. HCA has a steering committee, and every case starts by restructuring processes and cleaning data.
- **`strategic-renewal/organizational-culture`.** Culture is one of the six levers. The AI-ready values extend Westerman's 2019 digital-culture values with ethics and empathy, and with openness to non-human collaborators.
