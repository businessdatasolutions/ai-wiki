# Richardson — AI at work (Reuters Econ World, Oct 2026)

> AI is already changing the labor market, but not in the way many people expect. Nela Richardson, chief economist at ADP, joins host Carmel Crimmins to explain why AI appears to be reshaping tasks rather than eliminating jobs, why younger workers are feeling the impact first, and why experience is proving more valuable in the age of artificial intelligence than many economists predicted.
>
> — channel description, Reuters (hashtags and links omitted)

## TL;DR

A ~31-minute episode of *Reuters Econ World* on the **[[Reuters]]** channel, published 7 October 2026. The host is **Carmel Crimmins**; the guest is **Nela Richardson**, chief economist at the payroll firm **ADP**, whose payroll records are the data behind [[2026-04-28-brynjolfsson-canaries-coal-mine|Canaries in the Coal Mine]]. The captions are auto-generated, with proper nouns corrected at acquire time. Reuters cut nine data charts into the video; they are verified and published below, and they carry most of the episode's numbers.

**1. "Better to use a microscope than a telescope" (1:22–3:06).** The changes are *"at the task level. They're very minute. You're not seeing them in the overall productivity numbers yet."* ADP and *"our partners at Stanford"* sort payroll data by occupation (AI exposure) and by career stage. The pattern: employment of young people in highly exposed fields (software developers, customer service reps) has fallen; young people in non-exposed fields such as home health and nursing aides have gained; and in older career stages AI exposure seems to *raise* employment: *"It's augmentative."*

**2. The latest figures (3:39–4:07).** In ADP's August report, year-over-year employment of young people in AI-exposed fields fell **4.4%**, and in non-exposed fields **2%**. Her reading is that AI *"seems to reinforce knowledge and expertise"*. Before 2026 her prior had been the opposite: firms would drop *"all the expensive older people"* and hire young people with the new tools. *"That's not what's happening."* The tasks that go are the ones *"that don't require experience, that don't require judgment."*

**3. A wage premium inside exposed work (6:00–6:39).** Within the digital job category, ML and AI occupations earn *"a huge wage premium versus other digital jobs."*

**4. Tasks, not jobs (7:24–8:15).** Her hypothesis: *"what we're going to be talking about in the labor market is how many tasks are created and destroyed, not how many jobs."* ADP has unbundled IT jobs into tasks: developing, design and creation are high-value; explaining, documentation and diagnostics are low-value. Some high-value tasks have lost value since ChatGPT, *"like explaining technological advances. There's AI for that."* Employers should upskill early-career staff to the high-value tasks rather than turn over the workforce in search of new skills.

**5. The human-capital argument (10:08–11:41).** Asked about the Jevons paradox and radiologists, she agrees AI could create jobs but says *"we're not on a preset pathway to that."* Her history: US labour productivity was lowest in the late 1970s and early 1980s, when the boomers entered the labour market as *"the largest unskilled workforce… in human history"*, and highest in the internet boom, when those workers had about 30 years of experience. *"We don't have 30 years to scale up young people to take advantage of AI. We have to do it now."* She calls this *"the missing part of the equation for why AI isn't showing up in the productivity numbers."*

**6. Mass layoffs as a one-off (12:09–13:24).** Possible, *"but only if people are shortsighted and they pick the lowest hanging fruit. The thing about using AI purely for an efficiency play is you can only do it once. You lay off your workforce, you have a great quarter, and then what?"*

**7. Where the wage premiums are (16:24–18:21).** In construction trades tied to the AI build-out, and in manufacturers upstream of it. The cause is demand meeting limited supply: older tradespeople retiring (*"10,000 people are turning 65 and older every single day"*) and less immigration. ADP matches individuals over time, so it can measure the pay rise of people who switch jobs: *"Premiums in construction are hitting almost 14% for gross pay."*

**8. Ageing matters more than AI right now (16:57–21:52).** *"If I could think of two A's that are most impacting the labor market right now, AI or aging, it would be aging hands down."* Three of every four net new US jobs in recent years have been in healthcare, driven by a large, wealthy retiree class that chooses to age at home.

**9. Inequality starts from real-wage losses (22:44–24:26).** Research with the University of Chicago: during the 2021–2024 inflation, **43% of workers saw real wage declines, averaging 8%**. Workers usually outrun inflation by switching jobs, and in a low-hire, low-fire market there is less chance to do that, *"they can't really capture the benefit of AI."*

**10. Trust (24:29–26:13).** Crimmins cites *"Anthropic's IPO prospectus"*, which asserts the technology will transform the economy more than electricity or the internet. Richardson answers that adoption depends on trust: *"if we have products that say that they're going to end humanity, people aren't going to trust it."*

**11. Advice (26:20–29:32).** Young people should lean into networking, informational interviews and *"going back to people and relationships."* Employers should find where their value creation lies and upskill toward it. On displacement: *"People talk about durable jobs. There's no such thing. But durable skills, that's something that we can build."* What may differ this time: *"you can use AI to help people displaced by AI."*

The episode closes with a quiz: Keynes's 1930 prediction of a 15-hour week by 2030.

## Dynamic-capabilities reading

- **`digital-transforming/improving-digital-maturity`.** Richardson's prescription to employers is workforce maturity: unbundle jobs into tasks, find where value is created, and upskill early-career staff to the high-value tasks rather than replace them. She calls missing investment in people *"the missing part of the equation"* for the productivity gap.
- **`contextual/external-triggers`.** The episode places AI among external labour-market forces that currently weigh more: an ageing workforce, limited immigration, two years of low hiring and low separations, and the data-centre build-out pulling construction wages up.

## Debates and supersession

- **Does the effect show in aggregate data?** This episode sides with the reconciliation [[ai-employment-effects]] proposed between [[2026-04-28-brynjolfsson-canaries-coal-mine|Canaries]] and [[2026-09-08-hatzius-gs-macro-impact-of-ai-gdp-productivity-jobs|Hatzius]]: the effect is concentrated by age within exposed occupations and does not show in aggregates. Richardson is ADP's chief economist, and ADP supplies Canaries' data, so she is not an independent check on that data.
- **Both exposure groups' young workers are shrinking.** The −4.4% (exposed) and −2% (non-exposed) figures, and charts 1 and 2, show that 22–25-year-olds lost ground in both groups. The gap between them, not the decline itself, is what is attributed to AI. Richardson also names a general low-hire market for young people (*"a tough labor market for young people for a variety of reasons"*).
- **A dating slip.** Richardson dates the ChatGPT release to *"October of 2023"*; it was November 2022, as the charts mark. Left as spoken in the transcript.
- **The OECD chart is a model.** It shows predicted contributions under three adoption scenarios, not measurements.
