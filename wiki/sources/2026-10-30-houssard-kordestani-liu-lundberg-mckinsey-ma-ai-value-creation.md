# Houssard, Kordestani, Liu & Lundberg (McKinsey) — M&A in Q4 and how AI is reshaping value creation

> As M&A enters the fourth quarter, dealmakers face a critical question: How can they finish the year strong while positioning their organizations for long-term growth? AI is reshaping the traditional M&A playbook, shifting the focus beyond transaction scale toward intelligent platforms and capabilities that compound value over time.
>
> In this episode of Inside the Strategy Room, McKinsey's Benjamin Houssard, Kameron Kordestani, Alex Liu, and Tobias Lundberg discuss the state of the M&A market heading into Q4 and how AI is changing the way companies approach value creation. They explore how dealmakers can move beyond a series of individual transactions to build a self-reinforcing growth engine.

## TL;DR

A 47-minute episode of *Inside the Strategy Room* on the [[McKinsey & Company]] channel, published 30 October 2026, host **[[Sean Brown]]**. Guests: **Kameron Kordestani** (senior partner, New York; leads North American merger management), **Tobias Lundberg** (managing partner, Sweden; co-leads M&A in Europe), **Benjamin Houssard** (partner, Paris; co-leads the global M&A AI initiative) and **Alex Liu** (partner, Minneapolis). Two halves: the market, then AI. Five claims:

1. **A market of mega deals.** Deals above $10 billion reached $1.1 trillion in the first half of 2026, up almost 140% on a year earlier and the strongest half on record: 36 such deals, against 60 in all of 2025, holding 42% of global deal value. Corporates lead; private equity, with record dry powder and holding periods about two and a half years longer, waits. Premiums are steady at about 26%; higher multiples reflect the sector mix, not overbidding.
2. **AI drives deals.** Seven of the year's ten largest deals are linked to AI, including utilities consolidating because *"data centers need power."* AI appears in the narrative of about half of all deals, but far fewer are pure AI plays: much of it is *"to get excitement around AI."*
3. **Four archetypes of AI M&A.** Core AI labs buying ten-person teams that solved one problem; tech-adjacent firms (IT services, software) repositioning, as in Synopsys–Ansys and Capgemini–Syniti; non-tech corporates buying capabilities (the most common for industrial and healthcare companies, e.g. Schneider Electric's many AI acquisitions); and the *talent acquisition* through hiring plus licensing, as when Amazon hired Adept's founding team.
4. **Integration as a lever for rewiring.** Using the integration to *"rewire"* the combined company can raise synergies *"by at least 50"* percent, in some cases double them. Three conditions: a leadership mindset to *"swing for the fences,"* AI talent, and a clean data foundation.
5. **AI inside the deal process.** Sourcing goes *"always on"* (patents, web signals, transcripts); due diligence covers all documents rather than the 15% a team could sample; integration runs on a library of agents per workstream. Awareness of AI for upstream deal work reached 88%, up 51 points in a year; adoption is lower.

## Key claims

### What AI changes for a deal team

Houssard lists five implications: redefine the target universe (*"it might be a 30 person startup that you have never heard about in a sector that you have never screened"*); talent is the asset and does not respond to a three-year earn-out but to autonomy and access to compute (*"if an AI expert doesn't have access to the right GPUs he might … go"*); speed over thoroughness; integrate lightly (*"you don't want to kill what you are buying with your corporate processes"*); and consider joint ventures, partnerships and licensing instead of full ownership. On due diligence: *"We could expect faster due diligence. What we actually see is deeper due diligence."*

### Where value shows up

Liu: *"a fair amount of experimentation"* and only point examples of *"hard dollar impact."* The mature use cases are software development (*"upwards of 45% productivity gains and 70% reduction"* in the end-to-end life cycle), customer service (virtual agents and after-call notes for human agents) and finance (invoices, closing the books, forecasts). He describes three levels: make a process faster; virtual agents doing human work; AI plugged into a whole ecosystem, such as inventory forecasting with automatic replenishment. One client is building an *"executive cockpit"* with agents that recommend direction to functions.

### Closing views

Kordestani: *"we're seeing a gap between those companies that are experimenting and adopting, which are now leading the pack, and those that are waiting."* He hopes AI can help understand two cultures before they combine; Lundberg, that it lifts integration capability, the one area where corporate deal skills have stayed flat.
